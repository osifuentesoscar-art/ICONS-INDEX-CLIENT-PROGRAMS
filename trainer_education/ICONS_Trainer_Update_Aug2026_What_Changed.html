What happened

An external evidence review checked 13 ICONS protocol elements against the 2018–2026 literature.

On 17 August 2026 Brace Life commissioned an outside review of the ICONS science layer — not a marketing exercise, a line-by-line check of thirteen protocol elements against current peer-reviewed evidence. Some elements came back confirmed and were strengthened with better citations. Some came back wrong, and those are what this module is about.

Every correction has already been folded into CLAUDE.md, into the document engine, and into Modules A, B and C. What has not been fixed automatically is the knowledge in your head. If you passed a gate in Module A, B or C before 8/17/2026, some of the answers you were scored as correct on are now retired standards.

Why this exists instead of "please re-read the modules"

Re-reading a 76-page module to spot eleven differences is not a remediation path.

Item 1 of 11

RIR default on primary lifts

Item 1 · Progressive Overload
Proximity to failure on a primary lift
Changes a rep call
Old standard — what you were taught
1 RIR on primary lifts

1 RIR was treated as the "hard working set" default for the day's main lift, on the reasoning that closer to failure drives more strength. 3, 4 and 5 RIR were taught as meaningfully distinct targets you could call in the room.

New standard — effective 8/17/2026
2 RIR on primary lifts

2 RIR is the default proximity for primary lifts. 1 RIR is now reserved for hypertrophy-priority accessory work. Everything above 2 RIR collapses into a single "technique / submaximal" band — not separate targets.

Why
A 2024 dose-response meta-regression found strength gains are largely unrelated to estimated RIR — so 1 RIR was never buying the extra strength it was assumed to buy on a primary lift, it was just buying fatigue. Separately, measured RIR accuracy is roughly ±1 rep in trained lifters close to failure and degrades the further a set is from failure — which is why 3 vs. 4 vs. 5 RIR was false precision.
Do this
Call "2 in reserve" on the main lift. Save "1 in reserve" for accessory hypertrophy work. Above 2, stop numbering it — say "this is a technique set, leave plenty in the tank."
Also new
Every new client now gets an RIR calibration protocol: on one submaximal set of a new exercise, have her call her own RIR, then take that set to true failure and record the discrepancy. Repeat until she's within 1 rep on two consecutive sessions before you trust her RIR calls to drive load progression. Treat a novice's RIR call as lower-confidence, not just noisier.
Item 2 of 11

LIFTMOR bone-loading protocol

Item 2 · Bone Loading
Intensity, supervision, and who is allowed into the protocol
Safety-relevant
Old standard — what you were taught
≥80% 1RM, 5×5, 2×/week

The intensity number was ≥80% 1RM, with no stated supervision requirement, no entry screen, no ramp-in phase, and no explicit prohibition on loaded spinal flexion.

New standard — effective 8/17/2026
>85% 1RM, 5×5, 2×/week — supervised, risk-gated, ramped in

The trial actually prescribed >85% 1RM. Four requirements now travel with it: supervision, a risk-stratification gate, a technique-first ramp-in, and a loaded-spinal-flexion prohibition.

Why
The ≥80% figure was simply a misreading of the source trial — Watson et al. prescribed 5×5 at >85% 1RM. The safety requirements come from the trial evidence itself: in a related trial, one participant sustained a vertebral compression fracture after a fall, with 30 falls across 21% of participants. Fall risk, not the barbell, was the proximate mechanism of harm — which is why balance/fall-prevention work belongs alongside the loading.
Supervision
Every supporting trial was fully supervised. Never send >85% 1RM 5×5 home as unsupervised homework for a postmenopausal client.
Risk gate
A client with a prior vertebral fracture, multiple low-trauma fractures, or very low BMD is routed to individualised physiotherapist input first — she does not enter the protocol on your say-so.
Ramp-in
Several weeks of technique-first, lower-intensity loading before anyone reaches >85%. The Teal (60%) technique day is the vehicle for this — for a bone-loading candidate it is a purposeful ramp-in phase, not a generic light day.
Prohibition
No loaded spinal flexion, and no repeated or end-range flexion, in any postmenopausal bone-loading program.
Unchanged
The framing. This is still "bone investment," never "heavy lifting." And a pelvic-floor flag is still not a reason to default her to lighter loads — 2025 data found heavy loading does not aggravate pelvic floor dysfunction symptoms.
Say this honestly if a client asks

The strongest supporting data for this protocol come from one research group's trial line. That's disclosed on purpose — it doesn't undercut the protocol, and being straight about it is house practice.

Item 3 of 11

Who actually gets the five-color intensity week

Item 3 · Intensity Framework
Novice rotation, and the Red (90%) day
Changes a week's structure
Old standard — what you were taught
The full 60/70/80/90/AR rotation is the ICONS default for everyone

Five colors, five intentions, applied as the house structure regardless of how long a client had been training. Red 90% days were a normal part of any client's week.

New standard — effective 8/17/2026
Novices run a simplified Gold + Green rotation. Red days need a reason.

A client in her first ~6 months runs a simplified two-day rotation — Gold (≈80%) + Green (≈70%) — hitting each major muscle group ≥2×/week. Red (90%) days are restricted to clients with a genuine testing or competition rationale.

Why
The periodization benefit behind the undulating five-color week is real but concentrated in trained clients — it's essentially absent in untrained ones. And no reviewed source supports 90% 1RM work as necessary for general strength or hypertrophy outcomes; the evidence-backed strength anchor is ~80% 1RM.
Not a safety claim
Be precise about this. Nothing found Red days unsafe for a recreational client — only unnecessary for the outcomes we're training for. Don't over-tell it as a danger.
Also new
Audit weekly volume, not just the intensity colors — roughly 10 sets/muscle/week for hypertrophy. A client can hit every color correctly and still be badly under- or over-volumed. And for older clients, add power work (30–70% 1RM moved with intent) into Green/Teal days.
Keep
The color system stays exactly as it is for client communication and adherence — it works. Just don't sell it internally as an "optimal" physiological distribution.

Item 4 of 11 — the most consequential correction in the set

The ALST scale

Item 4 · ALST Index
The women's tier structure was built on a male cutoff
Highest impact
Old standard — what you were taught
3 tiers: <5.5 At-Risk · 5.5–6.99 Normal, monitor · ≥7.0 Optimal

Presented as one severity ladder for women, with ≥7.0 kg/m² as the top tier to aim for and 5.5–6.99 as a middling "keep an eye on it" band.

New standard — effective 8/17/2026
2 tiers: <5.5 At-Risk · ≥5.5 within normal reference range

No "Optimal" tier exists for women. Above 5.5 is simply normal. ALST is a trend metric with a stated reference floor, not a graded score of how good her muscle mass is.

Why
7.0 kg/m² is EWGSOP2's male at-risk cutoff. It was never a female "optimal" number. The two figures aren't rungs on one ladder — they're two different sexes' thresholds. 7.0 sits roughly 1.5 kg/m² above where a healthy woman's ALST typically falls, so the old scale was simultaneously telling normal women they were merely "monitor" and holding out an essentially unreachable target derived from men.
Do this
A woman at 5.8, or 6.2, or 6.9 is normal. Say that. Never tell a female client she's "not optimal yet" on ALST — there is nothing above normal to reach for on this metric.
Unchanged
<5.5 kg/m² is still At-Risk for women and still shifts the whole program: resistance priority every session, protein at the top of the range, creatine strongly indicated, protein reminder on every page. The male cutoff is still <7.0 — that number was always correct for men.
Deeper point
Lean mass was never the primary sarcopenia criterion anyway. In the actual consensus algorithm, low strength is primary and lean mass only confirms it. Two zero-cost function screens now sit alongside ALST: grip strength (flag <16 kg women / <27 kg men) and the five-times chair-stand (flag >15 s). And note honestly: the scanner's own published validation never validated ALM/ALMI at all.
Item 5 of 11

The asymmetry trigger

Item 5 · Asymmetry Protocol
When a left/right gap actually counts as asymmetry
Changes who gets a protocol
Old standard — what you were taught
Any L/R gap ≥ 0.5 lbs absolute

Half a pound of lean-soft-tissue difference between limbs triggered the full asymmetry protocol — weaker side leads unilateral work, suitcase carry in the weaker hand, logged separately.

New standard — effective 8/17/2026
A ≥ 10% relative gap between limbs

Compute the gap as a percentage of the larger side. Below 10%, note it and move on — no protocol. It must also exceed our own measured test-retest variation for that scan region.

Why
0.5 lb ≈ 0.23 kg. The scanner's own measurement error on female leg lean mass runs roughly 0.61–0.69 kg — two to three times larger than the entire trigger. The old trigger was firing on measurement noise most of the time. Meanwhile the injury literature that actually links asymmetry to outcomes uses 10–15% thresholds — an order of magnitude up.
Do the math
Left leg 12.9 lbs, right leg 13.5 lbs → gap 0.6 lb → 0.6 / 13.5 = 4.4% → below trigger, no unilateral-lead protocol. Under the old rule this same client got a full protocol. Left arm 6.2, right arm 7.0 → 0.8 / 7.0 = 11.4% → clears the trigger.
Prefer
Where you have one, a functional strength/power test (single-leg press, isometric strength, single-leg jump) is the better primary trigger — that's where the evidence-linked thresholds actually come from. Use the scan for client-facing progress visualisation, where it's far less error-sensitive.
Honest framing
"Weaker side leads" is a defensible coaching convention — it caps the stronger side's volume at what the weaker side can match — not a research-backed rule. Present it that way. The 8-week corrective dose (6–10 weeks of unilateral work, 1–2 sessions/week) is evidence-backed.
Watch out
The engine's weakerSide() helper still only tells you which side is lower — it does not check the 10% threshold. Computing the percentage is a manual step. A non-"even" result is not confirmation the trigger is met.
Item 6 of 11

VFA (Visceral Fat Area)

Item 6 · VFA
From a four-tier risk table to a personal trend line
Changes what you say
Old standard — what you were taught
<70 Very Low · 70–99 Low · 100–149 Moderate · ≥150 High Risk

A four-tier risk-band table you could read a client's number against and give her a risk classification on the spot.

New standard — effective 8/17/2026
Retired. VFA is a personal trend metric.

Track her change over time. Don't classify an absolute reading against a risk table. Waist circumference is now the primary clinical-facing metric.

Why
No consensus body endorses a single VFA threshold — published cutoffs span roughly 82–140 cm². In women specifically, elevated-risk thresholds run higher than the old table assumed (around 106 cm²+, not 100), and one large cohort's median female VFA was ~90 cm² — meaning a perfectly typical woman sat near the top of our old "Low" band. On top of that, this scanner's VFA output was validated against DXA in kilograms, never against CT in the cm² units we were reading it in.
Do this
"This is your starting number — what matters is where it goes over the next few months." Add a tape measure to intake and use waist circumference against the international consensus female thresholds (≥80 / 90 / 105 / 115 cm) as the clinically-endorsed metric. A short low-stakes tag like "Very Low" is still acceptable if the methodology caveat travels with it every time.
Never
Never present a VFA reading alone as a cardiometabolic verdict.
Item 7 of 11

What a Styku scan is actually good for

Item 7 · Scan Validity
A trend tool, not a diagnostic — and segmental data is not precise
Changes what you claim
Old standard — what was implied
Scan numbers read as precise measurements of this client's body

Absolute values — body fat %, per-limb lean mass — were quoted to clients as findings, with no stated uncertainty around any single reading.

New standard — effective 8/17/2026
Excellent precision scan-to-scan. Meaningful error on any one absolute reading.

Present every absolute number as a trend indicator with individual-level uncertainty. Segmental (per-limb) composition is not reliable enough to report as precise — full stop.

The numbers
Repeat scans on the same person agree tightly — well under 1 point on body fat %. But a single scan's agreement with a clinical DXA reading spans roughly 7–11 percentage points of body fat for an individual. Against a true four-compartment criterion, validity is poor. Segmental agreement with DXA is poor enough to be unsuitable for client-facing precision claims.
Where it shines
Change-tracking — the actual reason we scan — performs well, and better in women. Sub-kilogram error on lean-mass change over a training block is genuinely useful. And circumferences are the single most trustworthy output the device produces.
Say it out loud
Publishing the uncertainty is a trust asset, not a liability — it's what explains to a client why a number moved. "Your body fat percentage might differ from a clinical DXA by several points on any one reading, but repeat scans on you are precise to well under a point — which is why we read the direction, not the digit."
Protocol
Precision is the device's real strength and only a tight protocol preserves it: same time of day, same hydration state, minimal clothing, hair up, same posture. Sloppy scan conditions throw away the one thing it does best.
Items 5, 6 and 7 are one finding wearing three hats

The asymmetry trigger, the VFA bands and the segmental-precision caveat all come from the same underlying fact: this scanner is a precise trend instrument and an imprecise absolute one. Any protocol that asked a single absolute reading to make a clinical determination was asking the device to do something it was never validated to do.

Item 8 of 11

Pelvic floor bracing

Item 8 · Pelvic Floor Protocol
The blanket breath-holding prohibition
Changes a mid-set cue
Old standard — what you were taught
"Never hold your breath" — a blanket rule at every load

Exhale on exertion, always, on every set, for every postmenopausal client on any heavy-loading day. Breath-holding was framed as simply wrong.

New standard — effective 8/17/2026
A graded model — bracing scales with load

Light/warm-up: free breathing. Moderate working sets: exhale on exertion — still the default cue. Heaviest working sets: a brief, controlled brace is normal and appropriate, and may include a short breath-hold through the sticking point.

Why
The 2024 POGP clinical commentary states outright that a permanent "no breath-holding" prohibition is "not appropriate." Intra-abdominal pressure strategy is supposed to scale with the demand of the lift.
Still an error
Sustained breath-holding across multiple reps or a whole set. That's what was always actually wrong — the old rule just over-generalised it down to loads where it didn't apply.
Don't over-cue
The pelvic floor is reflexive. If a client is asymptomatic and isn't reporting anything, you do not need to cue a pelvic-floor contraction on every brace. Cue a pre-brace contraction specifically when leakage comes with a bearing-down sensation.
Symptomatic client
Exhale-on-exertion is a symptom-triggered tool, not a life sentence. Accumulate volume below her symptom threshold first, then progressively trial a stronger graded brace as coordination improves — don't park her at exhale-on-exertion forever once she's ready to progress.
Screen first
Braced breath-holds are low-risk in the absence of significant cardiovascular disease. If a client repeatedly experiences presyncope, syncope, or subconjunctival haemorrhage, switch her to exhale-on-exertion only and refer for cardiac assessment.
Unchanged — word for word
"If you experience any leaking, heaviness, or pressure — stop and flag your coach. This is common and treatable." Never "train through it." Never minimise it. Refer to pelvic floor PT when symptomatic — and note that heavy lifting co-activates the pelvic floor but does not strengthen it, so her lifting program is not a substitute for targeted PFM work.
Prevalence — why we screen rather than wait

Reported symptom rates in lifting populations run high: urinary incontinence in ~44% of female powerlifters and ~50% of elite powerlifters/weightlifters. At that prevalence, waiting for a client to volunteer symptoms will miss most cases. Ask at intake and periodically — which movements, at what load, at what point in the lift.

Item 9 of 11

The knee / ACL corrective circuit

Item 9 · Neuromuscular Circuit
From screen-gated to universal
Changes every client's week
Old standard — what you were taught
Run the circuit if she shows valgus on the screen

Watch the single-leg squat or drop landing, look for the knee collapsing inward, and program the corrective circuit for the clients who visibly failed.

New standard — effective 8/17/2026
Universal — every client gets it, dosed properly

20–30 minutes, 1–2×/week, sustained beyond 6 months, with a ≥76% session-completion adherence target tracked per client. A standing program component with its own weekly time allocation.

Why
Visual screening doesn't predict injury. A prospective study of 880 elite female athletes concluded visual assessment of the drop jump and single-leg squat "cannot predict who will sustain a future noncontact ACL injury," and a separate study found no association between inward knee motion and future injury across 74 real ACL injuries. Meanwhile the intervention works at the population level regardless of screen status. Net effect of the old rule: we were withholding an effective intervention from most of the clients who would actually go on to get hurt.
Adherence is the lever
High adherence (≥76% of prescribed sessions) is associated with a 64% injury-risk reduction, dose-responsively. Track it the way you track load progression. A short circuit she actually completes every time beats an elaborate one that gets dropped when the session runs long.
Add
A Nordic / eccentric hamstring component where it isn't already present — roughly halves hamstring-injury rates, and it's a different muscle group and mechanism from the Copenhagen plank, so it's an addition, not a swap.
Keep the screen
Still useful as a coaching tool for correcting an obvious form fault. Just stop describing it as risk prediction — it isn't one.
Framing
Describe it to clients as general lower-limb injury and knee-pain prevention — that's where the strongest evidence sits (a ~75% reduction in anterior knee pain) — not as ACL-specific risk screening.
Item 10 of 11

Protein targets

Item 10 · Protein
From age bands to context
Changes a nutrition target
Old standard — what you were taught
1.6 g/kg → 1.8–2.0 at 40+ → 2.0–2.2 at 50+

An age-banded escalation: a client's target stepped up as she crossed 40 and again at 50, on the assumption that the menopause transition raises protein requirement. Per meal, ~0.4 g/kg.

New standard — effective 8/17/2026
1.6 g/kg/day baseline · up toward 2.2 only for context

Move up within 1.6–2.2 g/kg for a genuine energy deficit, a heavy training load, or ALST At-Risk — not for turning 50. Per meal, ~0.3 g/kg (≈25–40g), 4 meals, 3–4h apart.

Why
The most current female-specific synthesis (Nov 2025) states plainly that peri- and postmenopausal athletes likely need no different protein target than premenopausal athletes, and reserves intakes above 1.6 g/kg specifically for energy restriction or heavy training load. Direct postmenopausal trial evidence for pushing intake higher by age alone is weak. The old per-meal 0.4 g/kg also exceeded both the position-stand serving guidance and the measured muscle-protein-synthesis-maximising dose.
Do this
Ask about her actual context, not her birthday. Is she in a deficit? Is her training load genuinely heavy? Is her ALST flagged? Any yes moves her up the range. A 52-year-old eating at maintenance with normal ALST and moderate training sits at 1.6.
New options
A pre-sleep 30–40g slow-digesting protein serving is a low-friction way to hit daily totals. Add ~10% to whatever target applies for a plant-based client. Treat the leucine-per-meal figure as approximate, not protocol-grade.
Know this
The document engine still computes the old age-triggered tier — the corrected trigger needs client-data fields that don't exist yet. So an existing client document may still show an age-derived number. Don't quietly "fix" one yourself; flag it, because every affected client needs an individual review, not a blanket regeneration.
Unchanged
Creatine. 3–5g monohydrate daily, no loading phase, with food, ~4 weeks to saturate, strongly indicated for women 40+/postmenopausal/ALST At-Risk. It remains the best-evidenced item in the whole ICONS protocol — and it must always be messaged paired with training, never standalone.
Item 11 of 11

Collagen

Item 11 · Collagen
Timing window and what it's actually for
Changes what you claim
Old standard — what you were taught
15g + vitamin C, 30–60 min pre-session — an acute performance aid

Framed as a pre-workout dose that acutely doubles collagen synthesis markers, positioned alongside protein and creatine in the supplement conversation.

New standard — effective 8/17/2026
15g + ~50mg vitamin C, 45–60 min pre-load — a chronic 12+ week support protocol

A connective-tissue support protocol taken consistently on training days over 12+ weeks. Ranks below protein and creatine in the client supplement hierarchy.

Why the timing narrowed
The source protocol dosed one hour pre-exercise at the 15g dose. The 30-minute end of the old window was never actually studied at 15g — the authors themselves noted a smaller 5g dose would have suited 30 minutes. Drop "30 min" as an equivalent option.
Why the reframe
A Jan 2026 umbrella review (16 meta-analyses, 113 RCTs) describes the effect as "chronic and structural rather than acutely ergogenic" — a positive effect on tendon morphology at low certainty, and no effect on tendon mechanical properties. A separate meta-analysis found collagen does not further improve musculoskeletal performance when added to exercise.
Do this
Don't promise a performance benefit. Position it as long-horizon connective-tissue support, and if a client is only going to do one or two things, point her at protein and creatine first. Mechanical load is still required for it to do anything at all.
