### Findings

#### Sub-question 1 — Does the protein literature attach a *measurable* definition to the energy-deficit condition under which it raises protein targets?

**Partial answer located; the numeric definition of "deficit" itself was not.**

The most explicit deficit-escalation recommendation in the protein literature is Helms et al. 2014, and it does not escalate on a deficit alone.

> Helms ER, Zinn C, Rowlands DS, Brown SR. "A Systematic Review of Dietary Protein During Caloric Restriction in Resistance Trained Lean Athletes: A Case for Higher Intakes." *International Journal of Sport Nutrition and Exercise Metabolism* 2014;24(2):127–138.
> **Population:** six included studies; adults ≥18 y, energy-restricted, resistance-trained >6 months, with lower body fat (males ≤23%, females ≤35%). Database search to July 2013.
> **Reported outcomes:** body fat percentage decreased 0.5–6.6% in all study groups; fat-free mass decreased 0.3–2.7 kg in 9 of 13 groups.
> **Recommendation:** protein needs for energy-restricted resistance-trained athletes are likely **2.3–3.1 g/kg of fat-free mass**, *scaled upwards with severity of caloric restriction and with leanness.*

Three operational consequences, each of which breaks the intake-field design ICONS was contemplating:

1. **The denominator is fat-free mass, not body mass.** ICONS's `proteinTargets()` computes from `client.weightKg`. Helms' range is per kg FFM. These are not interchangeable and the conversion is client-specific. Note a favourable wrinkle: total fat-free mass is the *best-validated* Styku output for this purpose — the peer-reviewed Styku S100 validation reported FFM with a test-retest CV of 0.75% (Bennett et al., *Clinical Nutrition* 41:211–218, already cited in the ICONS science layer), whereas ALM/ALMI was never validated in that study at all. If ICONS ever does move to an FFM-denominated protein target, it would be standing on firmer measurement ground than its existing ALST trigger does.
2. **The trigger is two-term, not one.** Severity of restriction *and* leanness co-determine the escalation. A single boolean `energyDeficit` field cannot represent this, and neither can a single g/kg output.
3. **The leanness term runs in the direction most coaches would not predict, and it excludes much of the ICONS roster.** Helms' inclusion criteria capped female body fat at ≤35%, and the review's stated logic is that *leaner* athletes need *more* protein in a deficit. Several documented ICONS clients sit at or above that ceiling (Kelly Mulroy, 36.4% body fat) and would have been excluded from every study in this review.

**Derived arithmetic, flagged as mine and not the paper's:** for a 65 kg woman at 35% body fat, FFM ≈ 42 kg, so Helms' *lower* bound of 2.3 g/kg FFM ≈ 97 g/day ≈ **1.5 g/kg body mass** — *below* ICONS's current 2.2 g/kg body-mass ceiling and below even its 1.6 g/kg baseline. For a lean 55 kg woman at 22% body fat, FFM ≈ 43 kg, and 3.1 g/kg FFM ≈ 133 g/day ≈ **2.4 g/kg body mass**, *above* the ICONS ceiling. The two systems are not comparable without a per-client conversion, and the direction of disagreement flips with body composition. Do not enter either derived figure as a threshold.

**What Helms 2014 does NOT supply, and this is the core failure of the pass:** no kcal/day deficit figure, no percentage below maintenance, no rate-of-weight-loss threshold, and no minimum duration was recoverable at the verification tier available. "Severity of caloric restriction" is left as a gradient with no scale attached. Whether the full text supplies one is unknown — see Gaps.

**Population transfer, stated explicitly:** resistance-trained lean athletes with >6 months training history, male-skewed, of unstated but likely young mean age. ICONS's roster is recreational women aged roughly 40–65, most peri- or postmenopausal, several with training histories under six months. **This is an athlete-derived, largely male-derived recommendation being considered for recreational midlife women. It is not a validated transfer and must never be presented as one.**

#### Sub-question 2 — Does energy availability (EA) supply a measurable construct a studio could populate?

**The threshold is real and its primary source was verified. Its transfer to the ICONS roster fails on the outcome measure itself.**

> Loucks AB, Thuma JR. "Luteinizing Hormone Pulsatility Is Disrupted at a Threshold of Energy Availability in Regularly Menstruating Women." *The Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism* 2003;88(1):297–311.
> **Population:** 29 regularly menstruating, **habitually sedentary**, young women of normal body composition.
> **Design:** 5 days in the early follicular phase; subjects expended 15 kcal/kg lean body mass/day in *supervised* exercise while consuming a clinical dietary product to set energy availability at 45 and either 10, 20, or 30 kcal/kg LBM/day in two randomised trials.
> **Finding:** LH pulsatility was unaffected at an EA of 30 kcal/kg LBM/day; **below** that threshold LH pulse frequency decreased and pulse amplitude increased. Disruption was more extreme in women with short luteal phases.

Four consequences, three of them disqualifying for the proposed ICONS use:

1. **The "~45 kcal/kg FFM/day optimal" figure is a study design parameter, not a finding.** In Loucks & Thuma 2003, 45 was the *balanced control condition* against which the restricted arms were compared. It was never derived as an optimum. Angle 2 flagged this as the highest-value provenance question of its brief and could not answer it; it is now answered, and the answer is that the number widely circulated as "optimal EA" is a comparator arm. **Do not write 45 kcal/kg FFM/day into any ICONS document as a target.**
2. **The threshold is anchored to an outcome that does not exist in most of the ICONS roster.** The 30 kcal/kg threshold is defined by disruption of LH pulsatility — i.e. by menstrual/reproductive function. A postmenopausal client has no LH-pulsatility outcome to protect. Applying a threshold to a population in whom its defining endpoint is absent is *structurally identical* to the ALST sex-conflation error this file corrected on 8/17/2026, transposed onto the age axis.
3. **The LH response itself appears to attenuate with age, well before menopause.** A companion paper surfaced in the same search: *"The response of luteinizing hormone pulsatility to 5 days of low energy availability disappears by 14 years of gynecological age"* (PubMed 16720651). Gynecological age is years since menarche; 14 years post-menarche is roughly the late twenties. **Verification tier: title-level record only — the title *is* the finding, and I have nothing beyond it.** If it holds, the physiological signal the 30 kcal/kg threshold was built on is already absent in essentially every woman on the ICONS roster. This must be fetched before it is either relied on or dismissed.
4. **The threshold is contested in the primary literature, not merely dated.** Also surfaced: *"Are menstrual disturbances associated with an energy availability threshold? A critical review of the evidence"* (*Applied Physiology, Nutrition, and Metabolism*, doi 10.1139/apnm-2023-0418) — title-level only; and a pilot study finding free-living competitive racewalkers and runners with estimated EA **<35** kcal/kg FFM/day showed peak serum progesterone indicative of ovulatory disturbance (*Frontiers in Sports and Active Living* 2023;5:1279534, PMC10690956) — i.e. under free-living rather than laboratory conditions the disturbance boundary may sit *above* 30. Neither was fetched; both are reported as contested-signal indicators, not as findings.

**Measurement feasibility was not researched.** Whether a non-clinical studio can compute EA at all — it requires a dietary-intake estimate, an isolated exercise-energy-expenditure estimate, and an FFM denominator — and what error such an estimate carries, could not be investigated (search budget exhausted mid-angle). Note the internal hazard already documented in CLAUDE.md: Styku BMR is below maintenance by definition, so any studio-side maintenance estimate built naively on it is wrong in a known direction.

**IOC REDs 2023 consensus:** citation shell confirmed (*Br J Sports Med* 2023;57:1073–1097) but **content not verified** — the direct PDF fetch was egress-blocked. Whether the consensus endorses numeric EA calculation for individual assessment, cautions against it, or routes to surrogate/indicator-based assessment via REDs CAT2 is **unknown to this pass**. This is the single most decision-relevant unfetched document in the report.

#### Sub-question 4 — Does the per-meal correction (0.3 g/kg women / 0.4 g/kg men) transfer across sexes?

This is the adjacent question the one productive angle actually answered. Its conclusion survives sustained attack from three auditors, though its reasoning does not.

**The strongest finding here was buried in that angle's gap list rather than its headline, and it is the one to carry forward:**

> **No study has ever administered multiple protein doses to both men and women and compared their dose-response breakpoints.** Every breakpoint analysis located is male-only. Both sex-comparative trials located used a single fixed 25 g dose. The question "do men and women have different per-meal MPS-maximising doses?" has never been directly tested.

All three auditors attacked this and none broke it. It is the correct headline for the sex-split question, and it is stronger and more defensible than the provenance narrative offered in its place.

Supporting verified material:

> Moore DR, Churchward-Venne TA, Witard O, Breen L, Burd NA, Tipton KD, Phillips SM. "Protein Ingestion to Stimulate Myofibrillar Protein Synthesis Requires Greater Relative Protein Intakes in Healthy Older Versus Younger Men." *J Gerontol A Biol Sci Med Sci* 2015;70(1):57–62. PMID 25056502.
> **Population: men only, both arms — zero female participants.** Older ~71 y, younger ~22 y. Retrospective pooled re-analysis of the same research network's prior trials, not a prospective dose-finding study.
> **Finding, confirmed verbatim by two independent auditors:** MPS plateaued at 0.40 ± 0.19 g/kg body mass (older) vs 0.24 ± 0.06 g/kg body mass (younger).
> **Precision caveat the "age axis" framing understates:** the older-men SD (±0.19 on a mean of 0.40) is nearly half the point estimate.

> Moore DR. "Maximizing Post-exercise Anabolism: The Case for Relative Protein Intakes." *Frontiers in Nutrition* 2019;6:147. PMID 31552263, PMC6746967.
> **Population:** re-analysis of published acute MPS trials in **young adults** of average body composition; underlying trial pool predominantly or entirely male.
> **Confirmed verbatim:** ~0.31 g/kg per meal of rapidly digested high-quality protein, and the author states directly that "potential confounders such as the co-ingestion of carbohydrate, **sex**, and amount of active muscle mass do not represent significant barriers to the translation of this objectively determined relative protein intake."
> **Also confirmed and materially important:** Moore distinguishes the muscle-specific bolus (~0.31 g/kg) from the dose required to maximise **whole-body** anabolism (**≥0.5 g/kg**) — a distinction ICONS's single per-meal field does not represent.

> Schoenfeld BJ, Aragon AA. "How much protein can the body use in a single meal for muscle-building? Implications for daily protein distribution." *JISSN* 2018;15:10.
> **Confirmed and upgraded from the source angle's "secondary-unverified" tier by two independent auditors:** the recommendation is 0.4 g/kg/meal **across a minimum of four meals in order to reach a minimum of 1.6 g/kg/day**, with the 0.55 g/kg figure derived as 2.2 g/kg/day over the same four meals.
> **This is a daily-distribution quotient, not a physiological per-meal ceiling, and provides no male-vs-female basis whatsoever.**

> J Nutr 2023;153(11):3173–3184, NCT03326284. "Dose-Response of Myofibrillar Protein Synthesis To Ingested Whey Protein During Energy Restriction in Overweight Postmenopausal Women."
> **Population — the closest match to the ICONS roster located anywhere in this pass:** 40 postmenopausal women, 58.6 ± 0.4 y, **overweight** (BMI 28.6 ± 0.4), n=10 per parallel arm; 5 days at ~800 kcal/day, unilateral leg resistance exercise, then 15/35/60 g whey; fourth arm 35 g under energy balance.
> **Confirmed to three decimals by two auditors:** myofibrillar FSR 0.043 ± 0.003 (35 g) vs 0.042 ± 0.003 (60 g, P = 1.000) vs 0.032 ± 0.003 %/h (15 g; P = 0.013 vs 35 g, P = 0.026 vs 60 g).
> **Carry this as 35 g ABSOLUTE.** Mean body mass was not reported in any record reachable, so no g/kg conversion is available. The "≈0.50 g/kg for a 70 kg woman" inference circulated in the raw findings is analyst arithmetic on an invented denominator and was refuted by two auditors — see the refuted section.
> **Transfer caveats:** overweight cohort (does not transfer to lean or underweight clients in either direction); ~800 kcal/day for 5 days is severe acute semi-starvation, not a sustained studio deficit; unilateral leg exercise; acute hours-scale MPS, not a training outcome.

**A limitation shared by every trial in this sub-question that no individual caveat names:** all of them measure **acute myofibrillar fractional synthetic rate over hours.** ICONS would be writing a **chronic daily prescription**. No source located establishes that a 0.1 g/kg/meal difference produces any difference in lean mass or strength over a 12-week block.

### Where this contradicts or corrects current ICONS guidance

Four items, ordered by severity. The first two are the highest-value outputs of this entire exercise.

#### 1. The 8/17/2026 protein correction rests on a narrower and more contested base than CLAUDE.md presents — and a peer-reviewed consensus body says the opposite

CLAUDE.md's Protein Targets section retired the age-banded escalation on the strength of the GSSI/Phillips synthesis stating peri- and postmenopausal athletes likely need no different protein target than premenopausal athletes. Three problems, all confirmed by at least two independent auditors:

- **The ISSN 2023 female-athlete position stand — peer-reviewed, and the only located consensus guideline explicitly scoping pre-, peri- and post-menopausal women — instructs that peri/post-menopausal athletes should aim for the UPPER END of the 1.4–2.2 g/kg/day range, and separately recommends a ~10 g high-EAA intact-protein bolus around exercise specifically "to overcome anabolic resistance."** That is a menopausal-status escalation from a consensus body. It was reported in the raw findings as *corroborating* ICONS's no-escalation position; two auditors independently established that it says the reverse. **Reporting the strongest counter-evidence to a shipped standard as agreement with it is the most serious analytical defect in this pass.**

"International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand: nutritional concerns of the female athlete." *JISSN* 2023;20(1):2204066. PMID 37221858.

- **The correction driver is industry continuing-education content, not peer-reviewed literature.** GSSI Sports Science Exchange is a Gatorade/PepsiCo publication; no peer-review process was documentable. Its ~0.31 g/kg per-meal figure is itself imported from Moore 2019's young-adult male-dominated re-analysis rather than derived from female data. **And nobody in this pass could fetch it** (`gssiweb.org` egress-blocked in three independent sessions), so no quoted figure from it has been confirmed by anyone here.

That is the honest state, and it is the posture this file already takes on creatine/bone and on LIFTMOR's single research line.

#### 2. ICONS's per-meal protein figure is internally inconsistent with its own daily target — arithmetically, verifiably, today

CLAUDE.md's corrected standard reads: baseline **1.6 g/kg/day**, per meal **~0.3 g/kg (≈25–40 g), 4 meals spaced 3–4 h apart.**

- 0.3 g/kg × 4 meals = **1.2 g/kg/day** — 25% below the stated 1.6 g/kg/day baseline.
- For a 65 kg client, 0.3 g/kg = **19.5 g per meal**, which falls *below* the parenthetical "≈25–40 g" in the same line. For the gram range and the g/kg figure to agree, the client would have to weigh 83–133 kg.
- The gram range is internally consistent with the daily target (25–40 g × 4 = 100–160 g = 1.54–2.46 g/kg/day for a 65 kg client). **The g/kg figure is the inconsistent term.**

**Root cause:** the 8/19/2026 engine change lowered per-meal from 0.4 to 0.3 g/kg on the reasoning that 0.4 "exceeded the ~0.31 g/kg MPS-maximising dose." But **0.4 and 0.31 are not competing values on the same axis — they are different quantities.** Schoenfeld & Aragon's 0.4 is a *distribution quotient* (1.6 ÷ 4 meals); Moore's 0.31 is an acute *MPS-maximising per-bout dose*, which Moore himself notes is **lower** than the ≥0.5 g/kg required for whole-body anabolism. Substituting a per-bout MPS optimum for a daily-adequacy building block breaks the daily total.

**Recommended file/engine change** (higher priority than the trigger work this pass was commissioned for, and unlike that work it is fully supported): restore the per-meal g/kg figure to **daily target ÷ number of meals** — 0.4 g/kg at four meals for a 1.6 g/kg/day target — and record Moore 2019's ~0.31 g/kg separately as an acute post-exercise MPS-maximising dose, which is a different field serving a different purpose. Alternatively, drop the g/kg per-meal figure entirely and keep the absolute 25–40 g range, which is already consistent.

#### 3. Two load-bearing meta-analyses carry post-publication flags that CLAUDE.md does not note

- **Morton et al. 2018** (*Br J Sports Med* 2018;52(6):376–384) — cited repeatedly in CLAUDE.md and the pivot of both the 8/13/2026 and 8/17/2026 protein passes — carries a published **Correction** (PMID 32943392, 2020). Not a retraction. Any figure taken from Morton 2018 must come from the corrected version, and the correction's existence should be noted at the citation.
- **Nunes et al. 2022** (*J Cachexia Sarcopenia Muscle* 2022;13:795–810) — the source of ICONS's 1.6 g/kg baseline — carries a formal published **Comment** (*JCSM* 2025, doi 10.1002/jcsm.70036) alleging inflated effect sizes from pooling multiple arms and pre-post SMDs, with an authors' rebuttal. **Contested, not retracted** — but this is the citation holding up the baseline number for the entire roster and it should carry the flag.

No retraction or expression of concern surfaced for any other source in the set (Moore 2009/2015/2019, Witard 2014, Macnaughton 2016, Mitchell/West 2012, Jäger 2017, Schoenfeld & Aragon 2018, Horstman 2019, Zaromskyte 2021, Sims 2023, J Nutr 2023, Wilkinson 2023, Mallinson 2023, Trommelen 2023, Apicella 2025, Helms 2014, Loucks & Thuma 2003). **Search-only check — treat as "nothing surfaced," not "retraction check completed."**

#### 4. The ~3 g leucine/meal threshold has no locatable primary source — the existing caveat is understated

CLAUDE.md's Protein Targets section carries "Leucine (approximate, not protocol-grade): ~3 g/meal from a complete protein source." Two auditors and the source angle each independently tried and failed to locate any primary human dose-finding study establishing that threshold. Two systematic reviews that examined the question directly both failed to identify one:

> Zaromskyte G, Prokopidis K, Ioannidis T, Tipton KD, Witard OC. "Evaluating the Leucine Trigger Hypothesis to Explain the Post-prandial Regulation of Muscle Protein Synthesis in Young and Older Adults: A Systematic Review." *Front Nutr* 2021;8:685165. PMID 34307436. — supports the hypothesis **only** in older individuals ingesting crystalline amino acids or isolated proteins; **not** for whole-food protein in healthy adults. ICONS's use case (real-food meals, mixed ages) is precisely the context where it does not hold.

> Wilkinson K, et al. "Association of postprandial postexercise muscle protein synthesis rates with dietary leucine: A systematic review." *Physiol Rep* 2023;11(15):e15775. — leucine dose associated with MPS magnitude in older but not younger adults; **no single plasma leucine variable had substantial predictive capacity in either group; no threshold identifiable in older adults.**

**Recommended file change:** harden "approximate, not protocol-grade" to **"no primary dose-finding source located; two systematic reviews examining the question failed to identify a threshold."** This is a strengthening of an existing correct caveat, not a reversal.

7. **No sex-comparative per-meal dose data in adults over 65.** Moore 2015's older cohort was male-only. ICONS's 65+ bracket has no sex-comparative per-meal evidence in either framework.
8. **No evidence located that the 0.31-vs-0.40 g/kg distinction produces any difference in long-term outcomes** (lean mass, strength). Every source measures acute FSR over hours.
9. **No validated proxy located** for a non-clinical studio to flag energy deficit without computing EA — not body-mass trend, not RMR ratio, not a symptom cluster.
10. **No published work located validating a flat-or-falling lean-mass trend as an energy-deficiency indicator**, nor establishing what magnitude of change over what interval exceeds measurement error. Note this proxy would be built on Styku ALM/ALMI, which was never validated in the device's own peer-reviewed validation study.

#### A practical note on the cost of the unfilled gap

If the trigger cannot be defined, the cost of *not* escalating is bounded and knowable, which is worth stating to a coach: for a 65 kg client the gap between 1.6 and 2.2 g/kg/day is 104 g vs 143 g — about 39 g of protein per day, roughly one additional serving. No located evidence establishes a chronic outcome difference for that increment in recreational midlife women. Leaving the trigger undefined is a defensible position with a small, quantifiable downside; inventing a threshold is not.

#### If a field is added anyway, add it as declared studio policy

This file has established precedent for exactly this move — the asymmetry test-retest CV threshold and the Styku rescan/DXA-pairing cadence are both labelled *reasoned internal choice, not evidence-based standard.* If Xolokan wants a trigger before the blocked angles are re-run, the honest construction is a **studio-policy field, labelled as such in the client document**, built only on things a studio can actually observe rather than infer:

- **Client-stated intentional restriction** (a yes/no the client herself knows and can answer, requiring no computation).
- **Documented body-mass trend over a stated window**, taken from studio scale data — an observation, not an inference.
- **Styku fat-free-mass trend across rescans** — the device's genuinely strongest output for women (female FM change R² = 0.86, ALM change R² = 0.70, already documented in this file's 3D Optical Scanning section).

None of these carries a validated protein threshold. **The escalation decision they inform would be coach judgment, and the document must say so** — the same posture already used for "weaker side leads" and for the Level field in the Assessment Report.
