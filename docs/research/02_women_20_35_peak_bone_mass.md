## Bottom line

**Do not use peak-bone-mass urgency as grounds to override a clinician-documented axial-load constraint.** The evidence that a 28-year-old woman's bone-accrual window is meaningfully open is a 21-year spread of irreconcilable estimates (~18 to ~40 years) resting substantially on *one* Canadian cohort counted three times. But do not swing to the opposite error either: "her hip window closed at 19, so nothing is forfeited" is refuted — a randomized trial *inside* this bracket (Kato 2006, mean age 20.7, femoral neck +2.6% in six months) and a 2024 meta-analysis of 19 jump trials (femoral neck +1.81% in younger adults) both show the hip remains genuinely trainable years past its age-at-peak. Age-at-peak-attainment and responsiveness-to-load are different constructs, and the timing literature cannot answer the training question.

**On the central substitution question, the evidence now points one direction and it is not the hopeful one: the lumbar spine is the least loading-responsive site in premenopausal women in every dataset located.** Jumping — the best-evidenced osteogenic modality in this population — significantly improves femoral neck BMD and does *not* significantly improve lumbar spine BMD (Zhao 2014; Journal of Sports Sciences 2024, non-significant in both younger and older strata). That resolves part of what the ICONS scoliosis section left open, and it resolves it as follows: **there is no demonstrated non-axial or impact substitute for axial spinal loading at the lumbar spine, because no located modality reliably moves lumbar spine BMD in this population at all.** Note carefully what this does *not* license — absence of demonstrated benefit from jumping is not evidence that axial resistance loading is dispensable, since no in-bracket resistance-training-versus-spine-BMD trial was located either.

**A finding that changes the stakes for this specific client, and that ICONS's scoliosis section does not currently contain:** adolescent idiopathic scoliosis is independently associated with generalized low bone mineral density that persists past skeletal maturity and into peak bone mass (Li et al., *Bone* 2020, n=550 AIS girls + 194 controls followed 1997–2016), with lower BMD still measurable in young adult women treated for scoliosis in adolescence (Courtois et al. 1999, n=33 vs 33). Her baseline bone status is plausibly *lower* than a spine-healthy peer's, not equivalent. This raises the value of getting a real DXA baseline; it does not change the load-vector answer, because no study of loading interventions in a scoliotic adult skeleton exists at any intensity.

**What a coach should actually do differently:** (1) stop citing peak-bone-mass urgency as a programming argument in this bracket — it is not citable at the precision ICONS has been implying; (2) prioritize hip-loading work, which is where the in-bracket evidence actually is, while recognizing that the *specific* modality with that evidence (jumping) is itself an axial spinal loading event at 2–6× bodyweight and is therefore not automatically appropriate for an axial-load-provoked client; (3) promise nothing about lumbar spine BMD from any modality; (4) treat AIS-associated low BMD as a reason to request a baseline DXA and route the axial question to the coordinating clinician with this evidence attached, rather than resolving it in the gym.

## Findings

### 1. When is peak bone mass attained? Site-specific timing, and the estimates do not reconcile

The consistent *directional* observation across three independent designs is that in women the **hip peaks earliest and the spine latest**. The *magnitude* of the timing estimates, however, spans 21 years and cannot be reconciled from anything readable.

**Baxter-Jones ADG, Faulkner RA, Forwood MR, Mirwald RL, Bailey DA. "Bone mineral accrual from 8 to 30 years of age: an estimation of peak bone mass." *Journal of Bone and Mineral Research* 2011;26(8):1729–1739.** n=197 (91 males, 106 females), University of Saskatchewan Paediatric Bone Mineral Accrual Study (PBMAS), healthy Canadian children followed to age 30, DXA at total body, lumbar spine, total hip, femoral neck, aligned on biological age (years from peak height velocity). **[ABSTRACT-VERIFIED — I reproduced the abstract text directly this session.]** Actual reported findings: total-body bone area increased significantly from −4 to +4 years from PHV; **total-body BMC reached a plateau on average two years later, at +6 years from PHV, equating to 18 years of age in girls and 20 in boys**; bone area plateaus 1–2 years earlier than BMC. Authors' own conclusion: *"Depending on the skeletal site, peak bone mass occurs by the end of the second or early in the third decade of life."*

> ⚠️ The version of this claim that entered the research batch — "+7 years from PHV," "18.8 and 20.5 years," "no bone area increase after +5 years" — is **wrong on all three numbers**. See *Claims that did not survive*.

**Population/transfer caveat, and it is the governing caveat for this entire section:** this is an **observational cohort of ordinary, untrained young adults**. It measures what happened by default. It does **not** establish that accrual cannot be *induced* by deliberate loading — a distinction the batch's own analysis correctly identified and then abandoned in its headline. Absence of accrual in an untrained cohort and "the window is biologically shut" produce identical data.

**Xue S, Kemal O, Lu M, Lix LM, Leslie WD, Yang S. "Age at attainment of peak bone mineral density and its associated factors: The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2005–2014." *Bone* 2020;131:115163.** n=18,713, US general population, cross-sectional. **[AUDITOR-RETRIEVED — reproduced verbatim by the citation auditor across independent searches; I did not re-query.]** Female age at peak BMD: femoral neck 18.7 yr, total hip 19.0 yr, lumbar spine 20.1 yr (males 20.5, 21.2, 23.6).
**Caveats:** cross-sectional generalized additive models fitted *across different people of different ages* cannot establish within-person accrual, and are biased by cohort/secular effects (documented secular BMD decline in US cohorts). The decimal precision (18.7 vs 19.0 vs 20.1) is not earned by the design. General US population — not athletes, not resistance-trained, no scoliosis subgroup.

**Berger C, Goltzman D, Langsetmo L, et al. (CaMos Research Group). "Peak bone mass from longitudinal data: implications for the prevalence, pathophysiology, and diagnosis of osteoporosis." *Journal of Bone and Mineral Research* 2010;25(9):1948–1957.** n=615 women, 527 men, aged 16–40, Canadian Multicentre Osteoporosis Study, longitudinal. **[AUDITOR-RETRIEVED — bibliographic record and all values independently confirmed by two auditors; both attacked the numbers directly and could not break them.]** Female lumbar spine PBM 1.046 ± 0.123 g/cm² at ages **33–40**; female total hip 0.981 ± 0.122 g/cm² at ages **16–19**. Male lumbar spine 19–33.

> **This is the single most favourable estimate for a 28-year-old and it must not be cited alone.** Two structural defects, both confirmed at abstract level by independent auditors. (a) **Definitional:** the authors define the PBM age range as *"the period during which BMD was stable"* — that identifies a **plateau interval**, not an accrual endpoint. A 14-year male window (19–33) confirms these are broad plateaus. (b) **Censoring:** the cohort is aged 16–40, so the female spine window's upper bound (40) *is the study's maximum observed age* and the hip window's lower bound (16) *is its minimum*. Both endpoints sit on the sampling frame. The celebrated "site dissociation" is partly an artefact of where measurement stopped.

**Naseri A, et al. "Bone Mineral Accrual From Adolescence Into Young Adulthood and Peak Bone Mass: A Longitudinal Cohort Study." *Health Science Reports* 2025. DOI 10.1002/hsr2.71411.** n=159, retrospective, **two assessment points per participant**. **[AUDITOR-RETRIEVED.]** Female peak total-body BMC 24.8 yr, peak total-body BMD 24.0 yr. **Caveat:** a two-point design cannot locate a curve maximum to within 0.8 years; those decimals are false precision. Cohort country/ethnicity not recovered, which blocks any transfer judgement. Total body only — does not adjudicate the spine question.

**Sagelv EH, Emaus N, Evensen E, Christoffersen T, Dennison E, Furberg AS, Grimnes G, Johansson J, Nielsen CS, Nilsen OA, Winther A. "Acquisition of peak bone mass in a Norwegian youth cohort: longitudinal findings from the Fit Futures study 2010–2022." *Archives of Osteoporosis* 2024. PMID 38960953; PMC11222189.** n=980 (473 female, 48%), three waves, ages 16–19 at baseline to 26–29. **[AUDITOR-RETRIEVED, with a bibliographic correction I partially corroborated: the journal is *Archives of Osteoporosis*, not *Osteoporosis International* as the batch stated — the Springer identifier surfaced in my own search is the `s11657` series, which is *Archives of Osteoporosis*.]** Female femoral neck BMD 1.070 g/cm² (1.059–1.082) at median age 16 → 1.076 (1.065–1.088, p=0.015) at 18 → **declined to 1.041 (1.029–1.053, p<0.001) at median age 27**; total body BMD continued rising 16→27.
**Caveat that matters most:** the sites measured were femoral neck, total hip and total body **only — lumbar spine was not among them**, so this study cannot speak to the spine question at all. And the observed femoral-neck decline is the natural history of **untrained Norwegian general-population women**; it does not describe a woman under progressive loading.

**Honest state of this sub-question:** for the female spine/total body, located estimates run ~18 (Baxter-Jones), 20.1 (Xue), 24.0–24.8 (Naseri), and 33–40 (Berger). **No consensus statement, meta-analysis, or methodological reconciliation of these estimates was located.** Any ICONS statement on whether a 28-year-old's window is open must present this as genuinely contested rather than selecting whichever figure supports the programming decision at hand.

### 2. Post-peak trajectories: BMC and BMD diverge — but the sex attribution is contested and unusable

**Zheng Y, Baxter-Jones ADG, Elhakeem A, Jackowski SA, Erlandson MC. "Site and sex specific bone mineral content, and density trajectories from adolescence through to 15 years post peak bone mass." *Annals of Human Biology* 2025;52(1). DOI 10.1080/03014460.2025.2540005.** n=162 (70 males, 92 females), SITAR models centred on PBM, four sites. **[ABSTRACT-VERIFIED for authorship, sample and design — I retrieved these directly this session.]**

Verified at abstract level: the cohort is **Canadian youth followed up to 15 years beyond peak accrual**, with **sex-specific increases in bone mineral content at total body, hip and lumbar spine of up to 7%, while bone mineral density declined at some skeletal sites.**

> **[CONTESTED — DO NOT USE IN EITHER DIRECTION.]** The batch reported "females gained 1–7% BMC at total body, lumbar spine and total hip while femoral neck BMC fell 1.3%." An independent auditor re-query returned the **opposite sex attribution** — males showing the −1.3% femoral neck BMC loss, females increasing at all sites. Two secondhand syntheses disagree about which sex lost bone. This single number is what the batch's headline rested on ("her hip is already declining"). **It cannot be used until the paper is opened.**

> **[CONFIRMED NON-INDEPENDENCE — a correction to how the batch presented its evidence base.]** My verification of the author list settles what the batch only suspected: **Adam D.G. Baxter-Jones is an author**, and the cohort is the University of Saskatchewan PBMAS. This paper, Baxter-Jones 2011, and Bailey 1999 are **one cohort, one DXA device generation, one analytic tradition — not three independent lines of evidence.** They must never be presented as corroborating each other. What looked like "four independent estimates spanning 21 years" is really two cohorts and two cross-sectional NHANES analyses.

**The interpretively useful part survives regardless of the sex split:** BMC and areal BMD **diverge** after peak — mineral content can continue creeping up (largely via continued periosteal expansion and growing bone area) while areal density falls, because area grows faster than mineral. This is a real interpretive trap for any client-facing document quoting a single DXA BMD figure as though it were the whole story. Flagged honestly: a divergence of exactly this shape is also what DXA positioning and area artefact produce, and no author discussion of it was readable.

### 3. Does loading still add bone after age-at-peak? Yes at the hip — and this refutes the batch's headline

This is the finding that overturns the batch's central inference, and it is the strongest in-bracket evidence located anywhere in this pass.

**Kato T, Terashima T, Yamashita T, Hatanaka Y, Honda A, Umemura Y. "Effect of low-repetition jump training on bone mineral density in young women." *Journal of Applied Physiology* 2006;100(3):839–843. DOI 10.1152/japplphysiol.00666.2005; PMID 16269526.** **[ABSTRACT-VERIFIED — I reproduced n, age, protocol, site, effect and p-value directly this session.]** n=36 **female college students, mean age 20.7 ± 0.7 years**, randomized to jump training or control. Protocol: **10 maximal vertical jumps/day, 3×/week, 6 months**. Result: **femoral neck BMD increased significantly in the jump group (P<0.01), reported as +2.6%**; no significant change in controls.

> **Why this is decisive.** Mean age 20.7 is **approximately two years past** Xue 2020's female femoral-neck age-at-peak (18.7) and past Berger 2010's female total-hip window (16–19). Bone was added at a site whose "window" every located timing estimate calls closed. **Peak attained ≠ unresponsive to load.**

**"Skeletal site-specific effects of jump training on bone mineral density in adults: a systematic review and meta-analysis." *Journal of Sports Sciences* 2024;41(23). DOI 10.1080/02640414.2024.2312052 (published online 2 Feb 2024).** 15 articles, 19 jumping trials, 18 trials contributing BMD data, **n=666 participants**. **[ABSTRACT-VERIFIED — I reproduced the effect sizes and confidence intervals directly this session.]** Femoral neck: **+1.50% (95% CI 0.83; 2.17)**, remaining significant on age sub-analysis in **younger adults (+1.81%: 0.98; 2.65)** and older adults (+1.03%: 0.02; 2.03). **Lumbar spine did not respond significantly.**
**Caveats:** author list not recovered — **do not cite this until authors are retrieved.** Adults of mixed sex and mixed age; the "younger adults" stratum is not confirmed to be women 20–35 specifically. Not retraction-checked (see gaps).

**Reductio available from inside ICONS's own science layer:** LIFTMOR (Watson et al., *JBMR* 2018) produced femoral neck BMD gains (+0.3% vs −1.9% control) in women aged **65 ± 5** — four decades past every "closed window" estimate. If age-at-peak meant loss of responsiveness, that trial could not have worked.

### 4. The central question — can non-axial loading substitute for axial at the lumbar spine?

**The evidence located answers a narrower question than the one asked, and the answer is unfavourable to the hopeful reading.**

**Zhao R, Zhao M, Zhang L. "Efficiency of Jumping Exercise in Improving Bone Mineral Density Among Premenopausal Women: A Meta-Analysis." *Sports Medicine* 2014;44(10):1393–1402. DOI 10.1007/s40279-014-0220-8; PMID 24981245.** Six RCTs. **[ABSTRACT-VERIFIED for the directional conclusion — I reproduced this session: "the sensitivity of skeletal response to jumping exercise in premenopausal women is significant and site-specific, with significant benefit from high-impact exercise noted, especially at the hip."]** **[AUDITOR-RETRIEVED for the numbers, from the DARE structured record:]** femoral neck WMD 0.017 g/cm² (p<0.001); trochanter WMD 0.021 (95% CI 0.018–0.024, p<0.001); **lumbar spine non-significant at p=0.181**.

> Two provenance notes, both honest. (i) I attempted to reproduce the p=0.181 figure and **could not** — the DARE record is egress-blocked and search snippets did not surface it. It is auditor-retrieved. (ii) One auditor initially retrieved a *conflicting* femoral-neck effect of 0.007 and traced it to cross-contamination from a different premenopausal impact-exercise meta-analysis before confirming 0.017 against DARE. (iii) The DARE confidence interval as retrieved reads "95% CI 0.014–0.20" around a point estimate of 0.017 — grossly asymmetric and almost certainly a transcription error for 0.020. **Do not reproduce that interval.**

**The lumbar-spine null is independently corroborated by a newer, larger, differently-populated meta-analysis** (*Journal of Sports Sciences* 2024, above — lumbar spine non-significant in both younger and older adult strata), and points the same direction as the pooled all-modality premenopausal meta-analysis below (lumbar spine effect marginal, lower confidence bound essentially touching zero).

**Kelley GA, Kelley KS, Kohrt WM. "Exercise and bone mineral density in premenopausal women: a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials." *International Journal of Endocrinology* 2013. DOI 10.1155/2013/741639; PMID 23401684; PMC3563173.** RCTs ≥24 weeks; femoral neck 7 studies/466 participants, lumbar spine 6 studies/402 participants. **[AUDITOR-RETRIEVED effect sizes — I attempted four independent searches this session and could not surface them, so these remain provisional:]** femoral neck **g=0.342 (95% CI 0.132–0.553, P=0.001)**; lumbar spine **g=0.201 (95% CI 0.009–0.394, P=0.04)**.

> The site asymmetry is the point. Reporting both sites as jointly "statistically significant" — as the batch did — flattens exactly what matters: **the lumbar spine's lower confidence bound is 0.009, functionally touching zero, on a marginal p.** Across every dataset retrieved in this pass, the lumbar spine is the least loading-responsive site in premenopausal women.
>
> **Erratum resolved [AUDITOR-RETRIEVED]:** the published erratum (*Int J Endocrinol* 2013, DOI 10.1155/2013/583217, PMC3793505) makes two corrections — a country name (Australia → United Kingdom) and **one P value corrected from 0.034 to 0.68**, a significance reversal. The authors state conclusions are unaffected; that is their assertion, not an audited fact, and which outcome the corrected p-value belongs to was not recoverable. **Retrieve the erratum before the parent paper.**
>
> **Scope caveat, unresolved and important:** "premenopausal" in this literature spans roughly 18–50 and is **not synonymous with 20–35**. The pooled age composition of both meta-analyses was not obtainable. The one trial age range that surfaced anywhere in the batch was 35–45 — entirely *above* the bracket. An adjacent premenopausal resistance-training trial (**Singh, Schmitz & Petit, *Joint Bone Spine* 2009, n=58, ages 30–50, 9 months strength training** — [AUDITOR-RETRIEVED]) found **no significant total-body or regional BMD change**, and its age range again sits mostly above the bracket. So "exercise builds bone in premenopausal women, therefore it builds bone during the 20–35 accrual window" is an **unjustified transfer in both directions** — the evidence may largely describe *maintenance* in women a decade past peak, which is a different physiological claim.

**The trap that survives every attack, and the sharpest analytic point in this pass:** the best-evidenced osteogenic modality in premenopausal women — jump/impact loading at 2–6× bodyweight — **is itself an axial spinal loading event.** For an axial-load-provoked client it therefore carries the provocation risk **without** the site-specific benefit, since it does not significantly move lumbar spine BMD. It is not a safe non-axial substitute and it is not an effective spine stimulus.

**What is genuinely unresolved:** **no source was located, of any design, comparing an axially-loaded exercise (back squat, standing press, conventional deadlift) against a non-axial or spine-unloading equivalent (hip-belt/belt-harness squat, leg press, hip thrust, chest-supported or machine hinge) with lumbar spine BMD as an outcome.** Nor was any source located on the mechanistic question that would determine substitutability — whether the vertebral osteogenic stimulus comes predominantly from external axial compression or from paraspinal/trunk muscle contractile force acting on the vertebral bodies. The nearest lead is a **musculoskeletal-modeling study (Liu S, Xia X, Nie Y, Huang M, Meng L, Du J, "Musculoskeletal model predicted paraspinal loading may quick estimate the effect of exercise on spine BMD," *Frontiers in Bioengineering and Biotechnology* 2024;12:1464067)** — but it is **n=10 postmenopausal women with osteopenia**, it is a *modeling* study predicting forces rather than measuring outcomes, and it is therefore exactly the "mechanistic finding dressed as clinical" category ICONS's own standards flag. **[ABSTRACT-VERIFIED as a lead only; it supports no claim.]**

### 5. NEW — Scoliosis and bone density: a body of evidence ICONS's scoliosis section does not contain

This was not in the research batch, was not in any auditor's material, and I located it this session. **It is the highest-value new content in this report for the live client**, and it changes the stakes without changing the load-vector answer.

**Li X, Hung VWY, Yu FWP, Hung ALH, Ng BKW, Cheng JCY, Lam TP, Yip BHK. "Persistent low-normal bone mineral density in adolescent idiopathic scoliosis with different curve severity: A longitudinal study from presentation to beyond skeletal maturity and peak bone mass." *Bone* 2020;133:115217. PMID 31891787.** **[ABSTRACT-VERIFIED.]** Prospective longitudinal, **550 AIS girls and 194 healthy controls, followed 1997–2016**. Finding: AIS patients had **poorer BMD, associated with curve severity, more likely to persist beyond the peripubertal period and at peak bone mass** compared with controls.

**Courtois I, Collet P, Mouilleseaux B, Alexandre C. "Bone mineral density at the femur and lumbar spine in a population of young women treated for scoliosis in adolescence." *Revue du Rhumatisme (English Edition)* 1999;66(12):705–710. PMID 10649605.** **[ABSTRACT-VERIFIED.]** **n=33 young adult women treated for scoliosis in adolescence vs 33 controls.** BMD values were lower in patients than controls; among patients, those with osteopenia had worn a brace significantly longer and had more severe scoliosis in adulthood. **This is the only located study in the population that actually matters here — young adult women with a scoliosis history — and it is small (n=33), French, brace-treated, and 27 years old.**

**Supporting/corroborating [ABSTRACT-VERIFIED at prevalence level, full citations incomplete — see gaps]:** osteopenia prevalence in AIS reported at ~30–36% at baseline across studies, with one longitudinal series reporting **86.0% of osteopenic AIS girls still had low BMD at skeletal maturity (age 16)** (PMID 17108402). Generalized (not merely local) low areal and volumetric BMD in AIS is documented in **Cheng JCY et al., "Generalized Low Areal and Volumetric Bone Mineral Density in Adolescent Idiopathic Scoliosis," *Journal of Bone and Mineral Research* 2000;15(8):1587–1595**. A 2022 surgical series (**Nishida M, Yagi M, Suzuki S, Takahashi Y, Nori S, Tsuji O, Nagoshi N, Fujita N, Matsumoto M, Nakamura M, Watanabe K, *Journal of Orthopaedic Science*, 2022, PMID 35985936**, n=61, mean 16.6 ± 1.9 yr) reports persistence of low BMD to bone maturity.

**Transfer caveats, stated plainly and they are substantial:**
- **Nearly all of this is ADOLESCENT idiopathic scoliosis** — a growth-phase population with curve progression as the framing outcome. ICONS's own scoliosis section already establishes that AIS evidence does not transfer cleanly to a skeletally mature adult. Only Courtois 1999 is in adult women, and it is n=33.
- **The association is observational.** Nothing here establishes that scoliosis *causes* low BMD, nor that low BMD in AIS is modifiable by loading. Confounders include bracing (which reduces loading — and the Courtois data associate longer bracing with osteopenia), reduced physical activity, and low calcium intake (one cited AIS series reported mean intake of 361 mg/day).
- **DXA measurement confound, unresolved:** lumbar DXA in a curved spine is measurement-confounded — vertebral rotation and asymmetric projected area distort areal BMD. ICONS's scoliosis section already flags this concern. **No source was located quantifying the magnitude of that confound, or establishing whether these studies' lumbar readings are interpretable.** A scoliosis client's own serial lumbar BMD readings may be less interpretable than a document would imply.
- **No study located, of any design, on bone-density response to a loading intervention in an adult with scoliosis.** This compounds the gap ICONS's sixteenth pass already recorded (no RCT of progressive resistance training in adults with scoliosis at any intensity).

**Practical consequence:** this raises the value of obtaining a **baseline DXA** for this client rather than reasoning from population norms, and it is the strongest available argument for taking her bone health seriously as a *monitoring* matter. It is **not** an argument for overriding her documented axial-load provocation, because nothing here shows that axial loading specifically is what would fix it.

### 6. How much is modifiable at 28? The two most-quoted numbers are pediatric and must not be applied to an adult

**Weaver CM, Gordon CM, Janz KF, Kalkwarf HJ, Lappe JM, Lewis R, O'Karma M, Wallace TC, Zemel BS. "The National Osteoporosis Foundation's position statement on peak bone mass development and lifestyle factors: a systematic review and implementation recommendations." *Osteoporosis International* 2016;27(4):1281–1386. PMID 26856587.** **[AUDITOR-RETRIEVED, wording confirmed.]** The paper's own figure is that **lifestyle choices influence 20–40% of adult peak bone mass**.

> **Two corrections to how the batch used this.** (a) The widely-quoted "60–80% heritable" is an **arithmetic complement**, presented in secondary restatements as a derived inference, not a statistic the position statement reports as its own. Heritability and "everything not attributable to the studied lifestyle factors" are not the same quantity — the residual includes measurement error and unstudied non-heritable factors. **Cite the 20–40% figure, which is the paper's; drop the heritability gloss unless the full text states it.** (b) Even the 20–40% figure is a **population variance-partition statistic** that places **no bound whatever on how much an individual can change** — a standard and consequential misreading. (c) **Scope:** this is a position statement on peak bone mass **DEVELOPMENT in childhood and adolescence.** Applying its modifiability share to a 28-year-old is outside the document's own remit.
> **Currency caveat:** no 2020–2026 update or replacement was located despite targeted searching, including under the organisation's current name (Bone Health & Osteoporosis Foundation). Cite it with the same *"most current version located, not confirmed unsuperseded"* caveat ICONS already applies to ACR 2019 and ACSM 2019. A **figure-attribution erratum exists (PMID 26935424) — it is a correction, not a retraction.**

**Bailey DA, McKay HA, Mirwald RL, Crocker PRE, Faulkner RA. "A six-year longitudinal study of the relationship of physical activity to bone mineral accrual in growing children: the University of Saskatchewan bone mineral accrual study." *Journal of Bone and Mineral Research* 1999;14(10):1672–1679. PMID 10491214.** **[AUDITOR-RETRIEVED; author order confirmed.]** n=53 girls, 60 boys. **~26% of adult total-body bone mineral is accrued in the ~2 years surrounding peak bone mineral content velocity** (roughly ages 11–14 in girls).

> **Pediatric statistic. It transfers to a 28-year-old as nothing at all.** The batch framed it as transferring "ONLY as context for why little modifiable capacity remains" — that framing still asserts a pediatric statistic quantifies her remaining capacity, which is precisely what it cannot do. **Neither this nor the Weaver figure belongs in a 28-year-old's document in any framing.** Same cohort as Baxter-Jones 2011 and Zheng 2025 — not independent.

## Where this contradicts or corrects current ICONS guidance

**1. The 20–35 bracket's peak-bone-mass framing has no citation trail and asserts more precision than the literature supports.** Verified locally by the batch (network-independent): `docs/Evidence_Based_Science_Womens_Strength_Training.md` contains **zero occurrences** of "peak bone mass," "mechanostat," "site-specific," or "axial." The bracket's bone bullets were written by analogy from postmenopausal evidence and have no internal evidentiary backing to fall back on. The bracket asserts a window that "closes permanently" without any citation for the age or the mechanism, and the located estimates span 18 to 40 years and cannot be reconciled.

**2. "Closed" is the wrong metaphor and should be retired from ICONS language.** It is a decelerating curve, not a door — and, critically, **age-at-peak-attainment does not imply loss of responsiveness to load** (Kato 2006 in-bracket; *J Sports Sci* 2024; ICONS's own LIFTMOR citation at age 65). Replace "the window closes permanently" with something defensible: *"by the mid-twenties most sites have plateaued in untrained women; bone remains responsive to loading well beyond that, at magnitudes of roughly 1–3% at the hip over six months to a year."*

**3. The scoliosis section's tradeoff paragraph needs a substantive revision, and the revision does not go the way it might have been hoped.** ICONS's sixteenth pass framed it as: *"routing around axial spinal load protects her symptoms but removes exactly the axial stimulus the peak-bone-mass window depends on, and that window closes permanently."* Three corrections. (a) **No modality located reliably improves lumbar spine BMD in premenopausal women at all** — including the impact loading that works at the hip. So there is no demonstrated spine-BMD benefit being forfeited, only an untested assumption. (b) **But this is an argument from absence of evidence, not evidence of absence** — no in-bracket resistance-training-versus-spine-BMD trial exists either, so "axial loading doesn't matter for her spine" is equally unsupported. (c) The correct honest statement is: *the specific benefit being traded away has never been demonstrated in any population resembling her, at any site, for any load vector — which means the tradeoff cannot be quantified and cannot be used to override her clinician's constraint.* The section was right to refuse to resolve it; it was resolving the wrong question.

**4. Add AIS-associated low bone density to the scoliosis section.** It currently has no bone-density content at all. Li et al. 2020 (n=550 + 194 controls) and Courtois et al. 1999 (young adult women, n=33) together justify **requesting a baseline DXA for a scoliosis client** rather than assuming population-normal bone status. State the adolescent-population and observational caveats explicitly.

**5. ICONS has no premenopausal analogue to LIFTMOR, and one must not be invented by analogy.** No %1RM threshold, set/rep scheme, session frequency, or minimum program duration for detectable BMD change from **resistance** training (as distinct from jumping/impact) in women 20–35 was located. **Applying LIFTMOR's >85% 1RM / 5×5 / 2×week supervised protocol to a 28-year-old is a postmenopausal→premenopausal transfer with no license under ICONS's own Demographic Scope Rule.** The one located premenopausal resistance-training trial (Singh 2009, ages 30–50) found no significant BMD change.

**6. Internal inconsistency to resolve: the ACL incidence multiplier.** The 20–35 bracket bullet cites **2.8× female incidence**; the corrected ACL section elsewhere in CLAUDE.md cites **~5×**. Two different multipliers for the same claim sit in one document. Neither was traced to a primary epidemiological source in this pass, and the "highest-yield at 20–35" rationale requires **age-stratified** female incidence data that was not located — if peak incidence is adolescent (15–19), the bracket's own rationale is misstated. Flagged for resolution, not resolved here.

**7. Blacklist entry.** The ubiquitous **"90% of peak bone mass is achieved by age 18 in girls"** figure could not be traced to any originating primary measurement across repeated targeted searching — every hit was a review, textbook, or clinical page restating it without attribution. **ICONS should not use it, and should flag it if it surfaces in a future draft.** This is the same class of untraceable-but-fluent statistic that produced the system's prior citation incidents.

## Genuine gaps — questions asked, no evidence located

1. **The central question is unanswered.** No source, of any design, comparing an axially-loaded exercise against a non-axial/spine-unloading equivalent with **lumbar spine BMD** as the outcome. In any population, at any age.
2. **No mechanistic source** on whether the vertebral osteogenic stimulus comes predominantly from external axial compression or from paraspinal muscle contractile force. This is the question that would determine whether substitution is even mechanically plausible, and it is entirely unaddressed by anything located. (The Frontiers 2024 modeling study is n=10, postmenopausal, and predicts forces rather than measuring outcomes.)
3. **No resistance-training BMD dose for women 20–35** — no %1RM, sets/reps, frequency, or minimum duration. ICONS has no premenopausal LIFTMOR and must not fabricate one.
4. **No head-to-head impact-versus-heavy-resistance comparison** for BMD accrual in this bracket.
5. **No source separating "biological ceiling" from "insufficient loading stimulus in ordinary young adults."** Every timing cohort located is observational; none applied a loading intervention. "The window is closed" and "nobody in these cohorts trained hard enough to test it" produce identical data. Kato 2006 is the closest thing to a counter-test and it is one trial at one site.
6. **No source quantifying residual accrual capacity by chronological age** — e.g. "X% of remaining capacity at 25/28/30/35." The literature reports ages-at-attainment and post-peak trajectories; nobody frames residual capacity as a decaying quantity by age.
7. **No reconciliation of the conflicting age-at-peak estimates.** No consensus statement, no meta-analysis, no methodological adjudication. Compounding: **two cross-sectional NHANES analyses of overlapping survey years return 20.1 years and a "second peak at 36–37" for the same quantity in the same population** — which impeaches the cross-sectional method itself, not merely the outlier.
8. **No study of peak bone mass accrual or timing in women with scoliosis**, of any subtype. The AIS evidence located is about *prevalence of low BMD*, not about *accrual timing or trainability*.
9. **No quantification of the lumbar-DXA-in-a-curved-spine measurement confound.** Whether a scoliosis client's serial lumbar BMD readings are interpretable at all is unknown.
10. **No DXA least-significant-change / minimum-detectable-change source.** Without it, reported gains of ~1–2% at the femoral neck cannot be judged as detectable at the individual-client level, and **no defensible rescan interval for a BMD-motivated program can be stated.** Do not invent one.
11. **Modifiers were not researched at all** — RED-S/low energy availability thresholds, amenorrhea and recoverability of bone lost, and combined hormonal contraception. **The contraception question in particular is a live scientific disagreement** and the batch was right to refuse to adjudicate it from memory. Note the asymmetry that matters clinically: these modifiers only bear on this client if the underlying condition is present, so the correct next step is **intake screening** (menstrual history, energy availability, contraceptive use), not applying an athlete-derived threshold by default.
12. **No source on whether these modifiers alter the skeletal response to loading** — i.e. whether an energy-deficient or amenorrheic woman gains less bone from identical loading. This is the single most valuable unretrieved item for the commissioned question.
13. **No premenopausal-versus-postmenopausal head-to-head** on resistance-training response under one protocol. Note ICONS already holds one adjacent finding pointing the *other* way (the 2025 Exeter RCT: strength/balance adaptations irrespective of menopausal status), so "postmenopausal defaults are wrong for a 28-year-old" is currently an **untested working assumption**, not an established one.
14. **RETRACTION SCREENING INCOMPLETE.** Retraction Watch, Crossref, PubMed and all bibliographic services were egress-blocked to every agent in this pipeline including me. General web search surfaced **no retraction or expression-of-concern signal** for Zhao 2014 or Kelley 2013 (which carries an erratum only), and continued citation of Zhao through 2023 — but **absence of a signal in general web search is not a retraction check.** Given that ICONS has already had to blacklist one retracted meta-analysis in an adjacent domain, **no review-level source from this pass may enter the science layer until a bibliographic service is reachable.**
15. **Incomplete citations that must not be used as-is:** author list for the *Journal of Sports Sciences* 2024 jump meta-analysis; full citation for the AIS osteopenia-persistence series (PMID 17108402); authors for the *Nutrients* 2024 lifespan analysis were recovered by an auditor (Li T, Huang G, Hou D, Cheng Y, Zhang T, Liang Y, Liu J) but not by me.

| Jump-dose block: "10–100 jumps/day, 3–7 d/wk, 4–18 months, 2–6× BW, >43 BW/s, producing 0.6–3.4% FN gains" | **STRUCK — uncitable** | Sole sources are two **non-MEDLINE-indexed publishers** (medsciencegroup.us / Heighten Science; austinpublishinggroup.com), with no author, year, volume or page recoverable, summarising unnamed primary trials. It was also the batch's **only** dose prescription and **only** magnitude range, so the asymmetry was dangerous. Note the real primary source gives a far *smaller* dose: Kato achieved FN +2.6% on **10 jumps/day, 3×/week** — the extreme bottom corner of the claimed envelope, meaning the range as written would license up to 10× the dose with evidence for none of it. |
| Annals of Human Biology 2025: "**females** gained 1–7% BMC at TB/LS/TH while **femoral neck BMC fell 1.3%**" | **CONTESTED — unusable** | An independent auditor's re-query returned the **opposite sex attribution** (males losing at FN, females gaining at all sites). Two secondhand syntheses disagree about which sex lost bone. The percentages failed to reproduce on re-search. This number carried the batch's headline. |
| Treating Baxter-Jones 2011, Bailey 1999 and Zheng 2025 as three independent lines | **REFUTED — single research line** | **[ABSTRACT-VERIFIED by me: Baxter-Jones is an author on the 2025 paper.]** All three are the **University of Saskatchewan PBMAS** — one modest Canadian cohort, one DXA device generation, one analytic tradition. They must never be presented as corroborating each other. |
| Berger 2010 as evidence that a 28-year-old's spine window is open | **REFUTED as inference (numbers stand)** | The authors define the PBM range as *"the period during which BMD was stable"* — a **plateau**, not an accrual endpoint. It is also the most favourable of a set spanning 18–40, i.e. exactly the figure a motivated reading would select. |
| Weaver 2016: "60–80% of variability in bone mass is heritable" as a cited finding | **REFUTED as a citation** | The paper's own figure is **20–40% lifestyle-modifiable**; the heritability number is a derived arithmetic complement presented in secondary restatements. Presenting a reviewer's subtraction as a finding of the source is a citation defect. |
| Weaver 2016 + Bailey 1999 used to argue "whatever remains modifiable at 28 is a fraction of a fraction" | **REFUTED — pediatric→adult transfer, compounded** | Multiplies a population-variance statistic (which bounds no individual) by a pubertal-window statistic (ages ~11–14, n=53 girls), both from documents scoped to childhood/adolescence. Neither belongs in a 28-year-old's document in any framing. |
| *Nutrients* 2024 "second female lumbar peak at 36–37 years" | **Correctly quarantined; should live in gaps, not claims** | Pooled cross-sectional NHANES across ages 8–85 cannot produce within-person trajectories; a "second peak" is far more parsimoniously explained by cohort/secular effects, body-composition trends, or degenerative change inflating lumbar DXA. **It also contradicts Xue 2020 — also NHANES, overlapping years — which puts female lumbar peak at 20.1.** That mutual contradiction impeaches the cross-sectional method, weakening Xue too. |
| Kelley 2013 reported as "significant at **both** femoral neck and lumbar spine" | **QUALIFIED** | True as stated but flattens the decisive asymmetry: LS g=0.201 with a lower CI bound of **0.009** on a marginal p, versus FN g=0.342, P=0.001. Plus an unexamined erratum correcting a p-value **0.034 → 0.68**. |
| Angles 3, 4 and 5's conclusion that these questions "could not be researched" | **REFUTED — tooling artefact, not a literature finding** | WebFetch is genuinely blocked (I confirmed independently). **WebSearch is not.** Every verification in this report — Baxter-Jones's real plateau figures, Kato's protocol and effect, the 2024 meta-analysis's confidence intervals, Zheng et al.'s authorship, and the entire scoliosis-bone literature — came from WebSearch alone. A blocked fetch tool is a reason to downgrade confidence, not to stop checking. **Filing this batch as evidence of literature gaps would have encoded a transient tooling state as a scientific finding — a worse and more self-perpetuating defect than an unverified citation.** |

**What survived and should govern any rewrite:** the epistemics, not the conclusions. Baxter-Jones's observational-versus-inducible caveat (which the batch stated correctly and then abandoned — it should be **promoted to the governing rule for this entire topic**); the cross-sectional caveats on Xue and Naseri; the artefact flag on the *Nutrients* second peak; the refusal to use the untraceable "90% by 18" figure; the observation that impact loading is itself axial through the spine; and the refusal to adjudicate the oral-contraceptive question from memory. That last is the single strongest judgement call in the entire batch and is exactly the standard this system requires.
