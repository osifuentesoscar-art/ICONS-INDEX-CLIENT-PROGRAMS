## TL;DR

- The single biggest evidence-practice gap is that women are systematically under-loaded and under-fed protein: the highest-quality current research (Roberts/Nuckols/Krieger 2020; the 2026 ACSM Position Stand; the LIFTMOR trial) shows women adapt at least as well as men to heavy loading (ACSM 2026 anchors strength gains at ≥80% 1RM; LIFTMOR's bone protocol went to >85%, supervised), tolerate more volume, and recover faster between sets — yet most programs prescribe them light weights, high reps, and cycle-based restrictions that the strongest evidence does not support.
- Menstrual-cycle "hormonal periodization" is far weaker than popular figures like Dr. Stacy Sims imply: the best umbrella review (Colenso-Semple, D'Souza, Elliott-Sale & Phillips 2023) concludes there is no reliable influence of cycle phase on strength adaptations; the defensible practice is to train hard year-round and autoregulate around individual symptoms, not to build programs around phase.
- The genuinely female-specific priorities are bone and muscle preservation from ~40 onward, protein sufficiency (≥1.6 g/kg/day, with the higher end driven by energy deficit/training load rather than by age or menopause per se — corrected 8/18/2026, see Finding 7), creatine (3–5 g/day), and pelvic-floor/ACL-informed movement — these are where the evidence is strongest and where most programming actually fails women.
- A recurring failure mode worth naming explicitly: **male reference thresholds get applied to women by default.** The clearest case is the sarcopenia lean-mass cutoff — EWGSOP2's 7.0 kg/m² is the *male* at-risk threshold, roughly 1.5 kg/m² above where a healthy woman's ALM index typically sits, and treating it as a female "optimal" target pathologizes normal female muscle mass (see Finding 6 and section 2). The same pattern shows up in waist-circumference and visceral-fat cutoffs, where female-specific values differ materially from the sex-neutral numbers commonly quoted (section 4).

## Key Findings

1. Cycle-phase training is not supported for strength adaptation. The 2023 Phillips-lab umbrella review found the supporting literature small, heterogeneous, and methodologically weak. Meta-analyses that do show effects (Niering 2024) find only small-to-medium effects favoring the late follicular/ovulation phase for peak strength — not enough to justify restricting luteal-phase training.
2. Women respond to resistance training as well as or better than men in relative terms. Roberts, Nuckols & Krieger (2020, JSCR): no sex difference in hypertrophy (ES=0.07, p=0.31); women showed greater relative upper-body strength gains (ES=−0.60, p=0.002).
3. Heavy + impact loading is the strongest bone stimulus. The LIFTMOR RCT (Watson/Beck 2018) used 5×5 at >85% 1RM twice weekly and improved lumbar-spine BMD +2.9% (vs −1.2% control) in postmenopausal women with low bone mass — safely.
4. Estrogen loss drives anabolic resistance and accelerated muscle/bone loss; transdermal estrogen therapy amplifies muscle gains from resistance training (Dam et al. 2021).
5. Creatine (3–5 g/day) is one of the best-supported supplements for women, with strength, cognitive, and possibly bone/sleep benefits, especially in peri/postmenopause.
6. Sarcopenia thresholds: appendicular lean mass index (ALM/height²) <5.5 kg/m² (EWGSOP2, DXA) or <5.4 kg/m² (AWGS) defines low muscle mass in women — and these are **binary reference floors, not the top of a graded scale.** EWGSOP2 publishes no female "optimal" lean-mass tier; its 7.0 kg/m² figure is the *male* cutoff and must never be used as a female target. Lean mass is also not the primary criterion in the consensus algorithms: EWGSOP2 leads with low muscle *strength* (grip <16 kg in women) and uses lean mass only to confirm, while SDOC (Cawthon et al. 2020) drops DXA lean mass from its sarcopenia definition entirely.
7. Protein needs are higher than most women eat: ~1.6 g/kg/day minimum (Morton 2018 breakpoint 1.62 g/kg/day, 95% CI 1.03–2.20), up to ~2.2 g/kg where an energy deficit or heavy training load justifies it, distributed ~0.3 g/kg/meal across ~4 meals, with older women showing a blunted response to feeding. **Corrected 8/18/2026:** the higher end is context-driven, not age-driven — the most current female-specific synthesis (D'Souza & Phillips, GSSI SSE #270, 2025) targets 1.4–1.6 g/kg/day for female athletes and reserves higher intakes for energy restriction/high training load, and reports that *no research exists* in postmenopausal master female athletes on which to base an age- or sex-specific escalation.
8. Female-specific injury risk (ACL ~2.8× male incidence) and pelvic-floor considerations require targeted programming that most general programs omit.
9. Rep range is not the driver of hypertrophy once proximity to failure (RIR) is matched, and proximity to failure has a negligible relationship with strength gains, which instead track %1RM/load (Schoenfeld et al. 2021; Robinson et al. 2024; ACSM 2026) — a flat "8–12 reps for everything" rule is retired doctrine, not current evidence; this is sex-neutral, not a female-specific finding, but corroborates the sex-neutral rep-range evidence above (Finding 2).
10. Postpartum return to training is no longer a single blanket clearance date. The 2025 CSEP/ACSM-endorsed postpartum guideline replaces "cleared at 6 weeks" with an individualized, symptom-based progression, and diastasis recti abdominis (DRA) is a long-tailed, not a 6-week-resolving, condition — present in ~33% of women at 12 months postpartum (Sperstad et al. 2016) and still present in a meaningful minority of women decades out (22–36% at 3–30 years, per a 2024 cross-sectional study). Structured exercise, including sequenced (not permanently excluded) flexion-pattern work, is evidence-supported treatment, not something to avoid pending resolution.
11. Vitamin D and calcium supplementation are not a default add-on to a bone-loading protocol for an otherwise-healthy client. USPSTF's Dec 2024 draft finds no net fracture-prevention benefit from standard-dose supplementation in healthy postmenopausal women (moderate certainty, D-grade against). Adding exercise to supplementation modestly improves BMD outcomes over supplementation alone (Bai et al. 2025), but the exercise arms studied were not confirmed at LIFTMOR-specific loading intensity. Calcium supplementation specifically carries an unresolved, contested signal for increased cardiovascular/coronary risk in postmenopausal women (~15% RR increase, Myung et al. 2021, n=43,178) — worth real caution, especially for a cardiac-flagged client, not a blanket recommendation.
12. Osteoarthritis is not a reason to default to lighter loading. Knee OA prevalence is markedly elevated in postmenopausal women (~3× age-matched men by radiographic definition per a 2025 analysis), but resistance training — including progressively heavier loading — is core first-line OA management per the 2019 ACR/Arthritis Foundation guideline, and a 2025 network meta-analysis (46 RCTs) shows dosing should follow the client's limiting symptom (sustained moderate-intensity for pain/function; shorter, higher-rep blocks for stiffness). The one real boundary: a 2024 NEJM RCT found training alone did not match total hip replacement's outcome once surgery was already indicated — that stays an orthopedic referral, not a training substitute.

## Details

### 1. Hormonal Periodization & Menstrual Cycle Training

**The popular model (Stacy Sims).** Dr. Stacy Sims argues "women are not small men" and recommends concentrating high-intensity and heavy strength work in the follicular (low-hormone) phase and shifting to lower intensity, mobility, and recovery in the late luteal phase, citing progesterone-driven increases in core temperature (~0.5°C), reduced plasma volume, sympathetic activation, reduced HRV, and impaired sleep. She also recommends pre-loading carbohydrate before hard luteal-phase sessions.

**What the strongest evidence says.** The most rigorous synthesis — Colenso-Semple, D'Souza, Elliott-Sale & Phillips (2023, Frontiers in Sports and Active Living), an umbrella review of meta-analyses and systematic reviews — concluded verbatim: "it is premature to conclude that short-term fluctuations in reproductive hormones appreciably influence acute exercise performance or longer-term strength or hypertrophic adaptations to resistance exercise training." They specifically criticized the methodological quality (poor cycle-phase verification, tiny samples, high heterogeneity, I²>80%) of the reviews supporting phase-based training.

The Niering (2024) meta-analysis (22 studies, 433 women) found only medium effects (SMD 0.60) for isometric strength favoring the late follicular phase and small effects for dynamic strength (SMD 0.14), with the early follicular phase (menstruation) being the least favorable. So there is a weak signal that the early follicular phase may be slightly worse for peak strength expression, but no strong basis for down-programming the entire luteal phase.

**Verdict and practice.** Train hard across the whole cycle. Use symptom-based autoregulation (RPE, reps-in-reserve) rather than calendar-based restriction. If an individual reliably feels worse in the late luteal/menstrual window, deload or reduce intensity for her, tracked over ≥3 cycles — this is individualization, not a universal law. Hormonal contraception flattens the natural fluctuation and makes phase-based programming moot.

**Perimenopause & menopause.** This is where genuine, evidence-based change is warranted. Declining estrogen reduces muscle stem-cell (satellite cell) regeneration (30–60% drops in animal estrogen-withdrawal models), reduces bone mineral density, and induces anabolic resistance. Sims' menopause guidance converges with the RCT evidence here: prioritize heavy resistance training (she suggests 3–5 sets of ≤6 reps with ≥2 min rest, ≥3 sessions/week), add impact/jump training and sprint intervals.

**HRT/estrogen therapy.** Dam et al. (2021, Frontiers in Physiology) — a double-blind RCT in early postmenopausal women (≤5 years past menopause) — found transdermal estrogen therapy amplified skeletal-muscle mass gains over 12 weeks of resistance training vs placebo. This suggests HRT may enhance the training response, though decisions about HRT are medical and individual.

### 2. Bone Density & Sarcopenia Prevention (40–60+)

**ALM/ALST thresholds.** Low muscle mass in women is defined by appendicular lean mass index (ALM/height²):

- EWGSOP2 (DXA): <5.5 kg/m²
- AWGS 2019: <5.4 kg/m² (DXA), <5.7 kg/m² (BIA)
- IWGS: ≤5.67 kg/m²

These derive from ~2 SD below the young-adult reference mean (Rosetta/Baumgartner). Obesity confounds these cutoffs — a normal-weight-looking woman can be "sarcopenic obese."

**Three things about these numbers that a coach gets wrong more often than the numbers themselves (expanded 8/18/2026).**

*First: there is no female "optimal" tier above the cutoff, and 7.0 kg/m² is not one.* EWGSOP2 publishes ALMI cutoffs of <7.0 kg/m² for **men** and <5.5 kg/m² for **women** (Cruz-Jentoft et al., *Age and Ageing* 2019;48(1):16–31). These are two sex-specific at-risk floors, not two rungs of one severity ladder. Treating 7.0 as a female "optimal" target is a sex-conflation error with real consequences on a predominantly female roster: 7.0 kg/m² sits roughly 1.5 kg/m² above where a healthy woman's ALMI typically falls, so a woman at 5.8 — comfortably within her normal reference range — reads as merely "monitored," chasing a number derived from men that most healthy women will never reach. Above 5.5 kg/m², the honest statement is "within the normal reference range," full stop. (This was precisely the error corrected out of ICONS's own compact science layer on 8/17/2026; it is recorded here because the underlying reasoning, not just the number, is what prevents it recurring.)

*Second: lean mass is the confirmatory criterion, not the primary one.* In EWGSOP2's actual diagnostic algorithm, low muscle **strength** is the entry point — handgrip <16 kg in women (<27 kg in men), or a five-times chair-stand >15 s. Low muscle *quantity* (ALMI) only confirms a diagnosis that low strength has already suggested, and low physical performance (gait speed ≤0.8 m/s, SPPB ≤8) grades severity. Note that a coach quoting "grip <20 kg" or "gait <1.0 m/s" for a woman is quoting EWGSOP1 (2010) or AWGS 2019, not EWGSOP2 — the values differ by working group and are not interchangeable. EWGSOP2 itself states plainly that "some cut-off points are arbitrary at this time." The Sarcopenia Definitions and Outcomes Consortium went further: Cawthon et al. (*JAGS* 2020;68:1429–1437) excluded DXA-derived lean mass from its sarcopenia definition altogether, having found it not consistently associated with falls, mobility limitation, hip fracture, or mortality in pooled cohort data, and defines sarcopenia by grip strength plus gait speed alone. Practical read: a hand dynamometer and a chair are cheaper than any scan and sit *above* lean mass in the consensus hierarchy — a studio measuring ALMI but not grip strength has the algorithm upside down.

*Third: the specific ALM number a 3D optical scanner reports is not a validated output.* See section 4 — the peer-reviewed Styku validation did not report or validate ALM/ALMI at all. Present a client's ALM index as a **trend metric against a stated reference floor**, tracked over time, not as a precise risk classification or a graded "how good is my muscle mass" score.

**Best exercises for bone.** Optimal osteogenic loading requires high-magnitude strain at high rates. The LIFTMOR RCT (Watson, Weeks, Beck 2018, JBMR) is the landmark: 101 postmenopausal women (65±5 yr) with low bone mass (T-score <−1.0), 8 months, twice-weekly 30-min supervised high-intensity resistance + impact training (deadlift, squat, overhead press at 5×5 >85% 1RM, plus jumping chin-ups with drop landings). Results: lumbar-spine BMD +2.9% vs −1.2% control (p<0.001), femoral-neck improvements, improved functional performance, excellent safety. This overturned the old belief that osteoporotic women shouldn't lift heavy.

**Four conditions the LIFTMOR headline is routinely quoted without (added 8/18/2026).** The ">85% 1RM, 5×5, 2×/week" figure travels well and its qualifiers do not, which is how a genuinely supervised clinical protocol turns into unsupervised homework:

- *Supervision is part of the intervention, not a detail.* LIFTMOR, MEDEX-OP, and the subsequent pelvic-floor safety analysis were all delivered as fully supervised sessions. The UK "Strong, Steady and Straight" consensus (*BJSM* 2022;56:837) independently states resistance exercise for bone health is "ideally supervised." Prescribing >85% 1RM 5×5 as unsupervised homework to a postmenopausal client is not the protocol that was tested.
- *Risk-stratify before entry.* A client with a prior vertebral fracture, multiple low-trauma fractures, or very low BMD belongs with individualised physiotherapist input first. The concrete reason is in the trial data rather than in theory: in MEDEX-OP, one HiRIT participant sustained a grade-2 L2 wedge compression fracture after a **fall** (possibly squat-associated), and 30 falls occurred across 24 participants with no between-group difference. Falls, not the barbell, were the proximate mechanism of harm — which argues for pairing the loading protocol with balance/fall-prevention work rather than for lowering the load.
- *Ramp in.* Both the UK consensus ("lower intensity exercise ensuring good technique is recommended before increasing intensity levels") and LIFTMOR's own progressive design put several weeks of technique-first work ahead of >85% 1RM.
- *Avoid loaded spinal flexion.* The UK consensus explicitly instructs avoiding loaded spinal flexion and repeated or end-range flexion in this population; BHOF cautions against forward-bending/twisting for very-low-BMD clients. This is a standing programming constraint, not a per-client judgment call.

Two further points of honesty. The impact component is part of the stimulus, not a garnish: HiRIT means heavy resistance **and impact** training, and a program running the barbell work without the jumping/drop-landing component is running HiRT and should say so rather than implying the full LIFTMOR stimulus. And the evidence base, though strong, is concentrated — the supporting trial line comes substantially from one research group (Watson/Beck, Griffith University/The Bone Clinic) plus that group's own clinic data, with no independent replication RCT located outside it. That doesn't undercut the protocol; it's the kind of thing worth saying out loud, consistent with how this file flags single-research-line evidence elsewhere. Set against this, the UK consensus is deliberately more conservative, recommending an 8–12RM formulation as the practical default outside a specialist clinic while acknowledging that higher-intensity supervised work likely has the greatest BMD effect. Read the two together as: LIFTMOR's intensity is the evidence-backed ceiling for a supervised, risk-screened client; 8–12RM is the safer default where supervision or screening is less certain.

One reassuring finding that runs against the cautious instinct: a 2025 MEDEX-OP pelvic-floor quality-of-life analysis (n=115) found HiRIT at >80% 1RM did not aggravate symptoms of pelvic floor dysfunction, with a preliminary protective signal in women with a history of pelvic organ prolapse. A pelvic-floor-flagged client is not automatically a reason to default to lighter loading — see section 5.

**Impact vs resistance.** Both matter and are complementary. Impact/plyometric loading (jumps, hops, drop landings) drives high strain rates especially at the hip; heavy resistance drives spine and hip loading via muscle pull. Walking/cycling/swimming are insufficient bone stimuli. Minimum effective dose from LIFTMOR: ~2 × 30 min/week.

**Vitamin D & calcium supplementation as a bone-health cofactor — not a free add-on for a healthy client.** This is the natural next question a LIFTMOR-candidate client asks — "should I also be taking calcium and vitamin D?" — and the current evidence is more cautionary than the popular assumption. USPSTF's December 2024 draft recommendation statement (public comment period ran through Jan 21, 2025; based on an AHRQ-commissioned evidence review of 20 RCTs, 8 conducted exclusively in postmenopausal women) concludes with moderate certainty that vitamin D supplementation — with or without calcium, at standard doses — has no net benefit for primary prevention of fractures or falls in asymptomatic, community-dwelling postmenopausal women and men 60+, and issued a formal D-grade recommendation *against* supplementing for this purpose in that population. This explicitly does not apply to a client with a prior osteoporotic fracture, a confirmed osteoporosis diagnosis, a condition causing vitamin D malabsorption, or confirmed vitamin D deficiency — those clients follow their prescriber's guidance, not this general-population framing.

Layering exercise onto supplementation changes the picture, but not all the way to "confirmed replication of our bone-loading protocol." A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis (Bai, Huang, Yan & Du, *Nutrients* 2025, 13 RCTs in postmenopausal women) found combined exercise + calcium/vitamin D supplementation significantly increased lumbar-spine and femoral-neck BMD compared with supplementation alone, with consistent but non-significant trends at other sites — worth knowing, but the exercise arms studied were heterogeneous and skewed toward modalities LIFTMOR does not use: whole-body vibration produced the most consistent benefit across both sites in subgroup analysis, and mind-body/traditional-Chinese-exercise programs (e.g., Baduanjin) drove the lumbar-spine-specific effect. None of the pooled trials were confirmed at LIFTMOR's specific ≥80–85% 1RM, 5×5, 2×/week heavy-compound protocol — so "add supplementation on top of our bone-loading program" is a reasonable extrapolation from adjacent evidence, not a directly-replicated finding, mirroring the same honest gap already flagged for creatine's bone effect below.

The unresolved risk signal that should make a coach cautious rather than casually encouraging: a meta-analysis of 13 double-blind, placebo-controlled RCTs (28,935 intervention + 14,243 control participants, n=43,178 total) found calcium supplementation — specifically in healthy postmenopausal women, isolated from the mixed-population data that shows a weaker or null signal — associated with a significantly increased risk of both cardiovascular disease (RR 1.15, 95% CI 1.06–1.25) and coronary heart disease (RR 1.16, 95% CI 1.05–1.28), on the order of a 15% relative increase; the effect concentrated at commonly-used doses (dietary calcium 700–1000 mg/day or supplemental calcium 1000 mg/day) (Myung et al., *Nutrients* 2021;13(2):368). The literature on this specific question is genuinely contested — other trials and reviews report no significant CVD signal — so treat it the same way this file already treats creatine's bone evidence: real, unresolved, and worth saying so plainly rather than smoothing it into false confidence either direction. This has a direct line to section 5's pelvic-floor/injury-screening caution and, more sharply, to any client carrying a cardiac flag — she is exactly the wrong client to be casually pointed toward calcium supplementation without her physician weighing in, given this signal.

**Practical read.** Food-first framing (dietary calcium and vitamin D intake, reasonable sun exposure) is uncontroversial and worth coaching regardless. A standing supplement recommendation for an otherwise-healthy, non-deficient, non-osteoporotic client is not well supported by current evidence as a fracture-prevention measure, and calcium's cardiovascular signal is real enough that a supplement decision belongs with her physician or dietitian, not as boilerplate coaching advice — this mirrors the referral-not-diagnose posture this file already takes toward HRT/TRT and RED-S elsewhere.

**Estrogen and muscle protein synthesis.** Basal MPS is similar in young men and women. Critically, older women show an elevated basal MPS rate but a blunted response to feeding ("anabolic resistance") — they become resistant to the muscle-building stimulus of protein (Gatorade Sports Science Institute review). This is why higher per-meal protein and heavy loading matter more, not less, with age.

**Current guidelines.** The 2026 ACSM Position Stand ("Resistance Training Prescription for Muscle Function, Hypertrophy, and Physical Performance in Healthy Adults," Currier, D'Souza, Fiatarone Singh, Schoenfeld, Smith-Ryan, Phillips et al., MSSE 2026;58(4):851–872), an umbrella review of 137 systematic reviews and >30,000 participants, states that voluntary strength "was enhanced by lifting heavier loads (≥80% one-repetition maximum), through a complete range of motion, for 2–3 sets... and ≥2 sessions/wk," while hypertrophy "was enhanced by higher volumes (≥10 sets/wk) and eccentric overload." Notably, training to momentary failure did not consistently improve strength or hypertrophy. The NSCA 2019 Position Statement for older adults (Fragala et al., JSCR 2019;33(8):2019–2052) recommends "2–3 sets of 6–12 repetitions at 50–85% of 1RM per muscle group," 2–3 days/week, with 1.5–3.0 min rest between sets; for frailty, progressing intensity from 20–30% up to 80% 1RM is well tolerated. Neither is sex-stratified, but both endorse heavy loading applicable to women.

### 3. Progressive Overload & Rep Ranges for Women

**Do women respond differently to rep ranges?** Largely no in relative terms. Roberts, Nuckols & Krieger (2020, JSCR, PROSPERO-registered): hypertrophy (12 outcomes/10 studies) showed no significant sex difference (ES=0.07, p=0.31); upper-body strength (19 outcomes/17 studies) showed a significant effect favoring females (ES=−0.60, p=0.002). The 2025 Refalo/Nuckols/Galpin Bayesian meta-analysis (PeerJ) confirmed similar relative muscle growth between sexes.

**Volume tolerance and fatigue resistance — a genuine female advantage.** Sandra Hunter's work (e.g., Acta Physiologica 2014: "Women are usually less fatigable than men for similar intensity isometric fatiguing contractions") establishes that women fatigue less, owing to greater proportional type-I (oxidative) fiber area, better perfusion, and less metabolite accumulation. Practically, women can often handle higher volumes and shorter rest intervals. Recent work (PeerJ) found female lifters recover faster than males between sets and "may be able to benefit from higher bench press volumes"; a 2024 squat study (BMC Sports Sci Med Rehabil) found "women recover better than men between sets in both exercises." Ansdell et al. (2019, J Physiol) found females demonstrate greater fatigue resistance at intensities relative to maximum force.

**Frequency.** No evidence women need different frequency; 2–3×/week per muscle group is well supported; higher volume tolerance means women may benefit from slightly more weekly sets (hypertrophy target ≥10 sets/muscle/week per the 2026 ACSM stand).

**Rest periods.** Because women fatigue less and recover faster between sets, coaches can use somewhat shorter rest intervals without the performance decrement men show — a practical efficiency lever.

**Rate of force development (RFD).** Women generally have lower absolute RFD and peak power; this supports including power/explosive and heavy work (not avoiding it) to develop the neuromuscular qualities that decline with age and matter for fall prevention.

**Rep range vs. proximity-to-failure (RIR) — should every exercise just be fixed at 8–12 reps?** No, and this is not a female-specific question but it belongs here because it directly extends the rep-range findings summarized above. None of the core citations below restricted or stratified their pooled trials by sex — this is general resistance-training evidence — but nothing in the literature reviewed suggests women respond differently on this axis, and it is fully consistent with the sex-neutral hypertrophy findings already cited (Roberts/Nuckols/Krieger 2020; Refalo/Nuckols/Galpin 2025). Schoenfeld, Grgic, Van Every & Plotkin (2021, *Sports* 9(2):32, "Loading Recommendations for Muscle Strength, Hypertrophy, and Local Endurance: A Re-Examination of the Repetition Continuum") re-examined the classical "repetition continuum" doctrine (heavy loads for strength, moderate loads for hypertrophy, light loads for endurance) and found comparable hypertrophic outcomes across a wide loading spectrum — roughly 30% up to 90%+ 1RM — provided sets are taken close to failure; load/rep-range itself was not what drove the hypertrophy result once proximity to failure was accounted for. Robinson, Pelland, Remmert, Refalo, Jukic, Steele & Zourdos (2024, *Sports Medicine* 54(9):2209–2231, "Exploring the Dose-Response Relationship Between Estimated Resistance Training Proximity to Failure, Strength Gain, and Muscle Hypertrophy: A Series of Meta-Regressions") sharpened this into a dose-response mechanism: hypertrophy tracked proximity to failure (lower RIR → more growth) largely independent of rep range, while strength gains showed a *negligible* relationship with proximity to failure — for strength specifically, %1RM/load is the driver, not RIR and not rep count. Practical read: locking every exercise into a flat 8–12-rep range would be a regression from current evidence, not an upgrade — it would cap strength-focused primary lifts at a range not suited to that goal, for no evidence-based reason. The defensible model is goal-varying rep ranges (an Isolated-zone activation exercise, a Compound-zone strength-focused primary lift, a Compound-zone hypertrophy accessory, and a Metabolic-zone conditioning circuit each have a different natural rep-range profile) with progression *within* whatever range is programmed governed by RIR/proximity-to-failure, not by chasing a fixed rep number. This directly corroborates the 2026 ACSM Position Stand already cited above (section 2) — the same umbrella review explicitly retires the "8–12 reps for hypertrophy" rule at the position-stand level and endorses a working range of roughly 1–30 reps/set depending on goal, and states training to momentary failure did not consistently improve strength or hypertrophy either, which is the other half of the same finding.

### 4. Body Composition & Assessment

**Visceral fat (VFA/VAT) thresholds — reframed 8/18/2026 from a risk classification to a trend metric.** The commonly quoted threshold is VAT ≥100 cm² for elevated cardiometabolic risk (Hologic DXA white paper; JASSO/CT-derived criteria; several datasets suggest a roughly sex-independent ~100–103 cm² value for metabolic syndrome), and the association it rests on is real — DXA-VAT >100 cm² tracked with ≥2 cardiometabolic risk factors (OR 2.2) in postmenopausal women, and DXA-VAT outperforms BMI and waist circumference for predicting cardiometabolic risk in postmenopausal (though not premenopausal) women. What does *not* hold up is treating that number as a precise classification boundary for an individual woman, for three separate reasons that compound:

*No consensus body endorses a single cutoff, and the published ones scatter.* CT-derived thresholds span roughly 82–140 cm² across studies. The ~100 cm² figure specifically has mediocre discriminative performance at its own boundary (sensitivity ~0.69, specificity ~0.62) — acceptable for population screening, weak for telling one client she has crossed a line.

*The female-specific values run higher than the sex-neutral number implies.* Kelley et al. (*Diabetes Care* 2003;26(5):1413–1420, "Visceral adipose tissue cutoffs associated with metabolic risk factors for coronary heart disease in women") found VAT ≥106 cm² marking elevated risk and ≥163 cm² marking substantially greater risk relative to women at ≤105 cm² — women in the 106–162 cm² band were ~2.5× more likely to have low HDL, while those ≥163 cm² were ~5.5× more likely, plus ~4× more likely to have a high LDL/HDL ratio and at higher risk of impaired glucose tolerance. A newer, directly on-population study sharpens this further and is worth knowing: Tan SYP, Narasimhan K, Wong BWX, et al., "Optimal visceral adipose tissue thresholds that minimize risk for osteoporosis and/or diabetes in midlife women," *Journal of the Endocrine Society* 2026;10(5):bvag049 (Integrated Women's Health Program cohort, community-dwelling midlife women) used spline-based modelling of the *opposing* effects of VAT — higher VAT raises diabetes risk while very low VAT associates with worse bone — to derive an optimal **range** of 46.5–116.6 cm², rather than a single ceiling. That framing matters for this population specifically: for a midlife woman, "as low as possible" is not the correct target, and a single-threshold model cannot express that. (The cohort is Singaporean, where high VAT at normal BMI is common; the authors flag the range's applicability accordingly.)

*The measurement itself is not validated in the units the threshold is written in.* Styku's own published validation compared its VAT output against **DXA VAT in kilograms** (roughly 0.1±0.2 kg bias, CCC ~0.81; Bennett et al., *Clinical Nutrition* 2022;41:211–218) — never against CT or MRI, and never in cm². Reporting a CT-derived cm² threshold from a device validated only against DXA in kg is defensible for tracking direction of change over time and indefensible as a precise absolute classification.

**Practical read.** Track a client's VAT as a personal trend rather than labelling a single reading against a tiered risk table, and pair it with **waist circumference** measured to protocol as the primary clinical-facing metric — that is the number an international consensus panel actually recommends for routine clinical use, with female-specific thresholds of ≥80 / ≥90 / ≥105 / ≥115 cm (BMI-stratified) per Ross et al., *Nature Reviews Endocrinology* 2020;16:177–189 (IAS/ICCR). Note the sex-specificity here too: the commonly quoted ≥88 cm female / ≥102 cm male cutoffs are a different, older convention, and neither set should be applied across sexes. If an absolute VAT number is shown to a client at all, state that it is a trend indicator rather than a diagnosis, that elevated-risk values in women run nearer 106 cm² than 100, and that the scanner's own VAT validation was performed in kilograms rather than cm². Framingham data show visceral fat's cardiometabolic association is genuinely stronger in women than men (β 8.36 vs 4.24 for the VAT–glucose relationship) — but that same analysis publishes no clinical cutoff for classifying an individual, which is the whole point.

**ALM index interpretation** — see section 2; clinically, <5.5 kg/m² flags low muscle mass warranting intervention, read as a reference floor and a trend, not as the low end of a graded scale.

**Styku / 3D scanning — what it is and isn't validated to do (expanded 8/18/2026).** The short version: excellent precision, moderate accuracy, unreliable segmentally, genuinely good at tracking change in women. Taking those in turn.

*Precision is the device's real strength.* The peer-reviewed Styku S100 v4.1 validation (Bennett et al., *Clinical Nutrition* 2022;41:211–218; n=188, 102 women, DXA-calibrated, vs. Hologic DXA) reported excellent test–retest precision — fat-free mass CV 0.75%, fat mass CV 1.94%, %BF RMSE 0.60, and VAT CV 3.67%, the last actually *better* than DXA's own VAT precision (8.61% CV). Repeat scans on the same person agree tightly.

*Accuracy for any single reading is much looser.* The same validation found a %BF limit of agreement spanning roughly 18 percentage points — a single scan can differ from DXA by something like 7–11 points in an individual client even though group-level bias is under 2 points. This is the classic profile of a good trend tool and a poor diagnostic one. Against a true four-compartment criterion rather than DXA, validity is worse still: Tinsley et al. (*Appl Physiol Nutr Metab* 2021;46:644–650) found poor validity vs. a 4C model, with %BF overestimated by 5.31 points vs. 4C and 4.20 vs. DXA alone. Because optical scanners are calibrated *to* DXA, agreement with DXA is partly built into the calibration — agreement with the underlying physiology is weaker than the DXA comparison alone suggests.

*Segmental (arm/leg/trunk) composition is not reliable and should not be reported as precise.* Segmental volumes agreed poorly with DXA in the Bennett validation (the two devices partition the body differently), with concordance correlation coefficients on the order of ~0.32–0.52 in comparable optical-vs-DXA segmental work. Any left/right comparison built on segmental lean mass inherits that error — see the asymmetry discussion below.

*ALM/ALMI was not reported or validated at all* in that study; the authors flag skeletal muscle mass explicitly as future work. A separate 3D-optical study (different device) estimated DXA ALM at r²=0.96 with RMSE 1.5 kg under cross-validation — and 1.5 kg of total-body ALM is roughly ±0.55 kg/m² of ALMI on a 1.65 m woman, i.e. an uncertainty band about as wide as the distinctions coaches try to draw around the 5.5 cutoff. This is the measurement-validity basis for section 2's "trend metric, not a classification" framing.

*Change-tracking — the actual use case — performs moderately, and better in women.* A longitudinal study (Fit3D, n=133, 45 women) found female ALM change R²=0.70 (RMSE 0.37 kg) versus male ALM change R²=0.52 (RMSE 0.52 kg), and female fat-mass change R²=0.86 (RMSE 1.98 kg). Sub-kilogram error on ALM change is genuinely useful across a 12-week block.

*Circumferences remain the most trustworthy output* (ICC ≈0.99), which is convenient, because waist circumference is also the most clinically endorsed metric in this whole section (see VAT above).

*A gap worth stating rather than papering over:* no source located gives a recommended calibration or DXA-pairing interval for optical scanning. Any studio policy on rescan cadence is therefore a reasoned internal choice, and should be described to clients as one rather than implied to be a validated standard.

**Left/right asymmetry from segmental scan data — a caution, not a protocol.** Because segmental composition is the device's least reliable output, a small absolute left-right lean-mass difference is very often measurement noise rather than a real asymmetry. For context on scale: the Shape Up! Adults study (n=634) reported 3D-optical-vs-DXA accuracy error of ~0.27 kg RMSE for female arm fat-free mass and ~0.61–0.69 kg for female leg fat-free mass — so a trigger set at a fraction of a pound sits at or below the device's own error, particularly in the legs. The injury literature that actually links asymmetry to outcomes works an order of magnitude higher: Bishop et al.'s review found 27 of 30 studies using a 10–15% asymmetry threshold, and prospective-cohort work links ≥15% strength/power asymmetry to injury risk. Two practical consequences: use a **relative** threshold (≥10%) rather than an absolute mass difference, and prefer a functional strength or power test (single-leg press, isometric strength, single-leg jump) as the primary asymmetry signal, since that is the modality the outcome-linked thresholds were derived in. Leading with the weaker side on unilateral work is a defensible coaching convention — it caps the stronger side's volume at what the weaker side can match — but no source located establishes that set-ordering itself changes outcomes, so it should be presented as convention rather than evidence. Corrective unilateral work does have a real evidenced dose: 6–10 weeks at 1–2 sessions/week reduced measured asymmetry in meta-analysed RCTs. One population-specific caveat carried over from this file's breast-cancer/lymphedema discussion: on a lymphedema-risk limb, segmental lean-mass readings can be confounded by extracellular fluid rather than muscle, so the scan should not be the sole determinant of "weaker side" for that client.

**Body-fat ranges for women (ACE/ACSM general categories).** Essential fat ~10–13%; athletes ~14–20%; fitness ~21–24%; average ~25–31%; obese ≥32%. Body fat rises with age at any given weight; realistic targets should be individualized and account for the fact that very low body fat impairs menstrual/bone health.

**Lean mass–bone–metabolic triangle.** In women 40+, higher lean mass tracks with higher BMD (muscle pull loads bone) and better insulin sensitivity/glucose handling; estrogen loss simultaneously worsens all three, making resistance training the keystone intervention.

### 5. Corrective Exercise & Movement Screening for Women

**Knee valgus & ACL risk.** Females sustain markedly more non-contact ACL injuries than males. A team-ball-sports meta-analysis (Sports Medicine 2022) reported non-contact ACL incidence of 0.14 per 1000 player-hours (95% CI 0.10–0.19) in females vs 0.05 (95% CI 0.03–0.07) in males (~2.8×); NCAA 9-year data show basketball 0.22 vs 0.08 and soccer 0.10 vs 0.04 per 1000 athlete-exposures. Females tend to injure in the frontal plane (dynamic valgus); males more in the sagittal plane. Drivers: hip abductor/external-rotator weakness (gluteus medius can't control hip adduction → knee collapses in), limited ankle dorsiflexion, hamstring:quadriceps imbalance, limb dominance.

**Screening does not predict who gets hurt — and this changes who should get the corrective work (corrected 8/18/2026).** The frequently cited basis for screen-gating a corrective circuit is Hewett et al. (2005, prospective), which reported that knee-abduction angle/moment on drop-landing predicted ACL injury with 78% sensitivity and 73% specificity. That study used *laboratory 3D motion capture* on a small number of injuries, and larger prospective attempts to reproduce it — especially with the visual, in-the-gym assessment a trainer can actually perform — have not held up. Krosshaug et al. (*AJSM* 2016) found the vertical drop jump a poor screening test across 710 elite female soccer and handball players. Petushek et al., "Drop Jump? Single-Leg Squat? Not if You Aim to Predict Anterior Cruciate Ligament Injury From Real-Time Clinical Assessment: A Prospective Cohort Study Involving 880 Elite Female Athletes" (*JOSPT* 2021;51(7):372–378) is the most direct test: 880 elite female athletes, 65 subsequent noncontact ACL injuries, trained physiotherapists visually rating single-leg squat and drop-jump control. Poor single-leg-squat performance appeared in 14% of the athletes who went on to tear an ACL versus 17% of those who did not — i.e., the screen was, if anything, marginally *inverted*, and the authors conclude clinicians should not rely on observational assessment of either test to identify at-risk athletes. Nilstad et al.'s "Kiss goodbye to the 'kissing knees'" (*Sports Biomechanics* 2021) reached the same conclusion on frontal-plane inward knee motion.

The practical consequence is not "screening is useless, so skip the correctives" — it is the opposite. The *intervention* has strong, high-certainty population-level evidence (see below) while the *screen* has none, so gating an effective intervention behind an uninformative test withholds it from most of the women who will actually get injured. The defensible model is to deliver the neuromuscular/corrective work as a **universal, dosed program component for every client in the relevant population**, and to retain the single-leg squat and drop-landing as what they genuinely are — useful coaching and technique tools for correcting an obvious fault in front of you — while dropping the claim that they forecast injury. Framing it as general lower-limb injury and anterior-knee-pain prevention (where the evidence is strongest and most certain) rather than as ACL-specific risk screening is both more accurate and easier to deliver honestly.

**Corrective protocols.** A 2025 PRISMA meta-analysis of RCTs (Annals of Medicine) found neuromuscular training "reduced overall knee injury risk by 22% (RR = 0.78, 95% CI 0.65–0.94, p = 0.008) and ACL injury risk by 50% (RR = 0.50, 95% CI 0.31–0.81, p = 0.005)," with high compliance (≥75%) the strongest moderator (RR 0.67). Effective components: lateral band walks (glute med), single-leg step-downs, box-drop stick landings (progress 15–40 cm), and jump-landing retraining. Females rely more on gluteal activation for knee control than males, so hip-focused work is especially high-yield. Lauersen et al. (*BJSM* 2018;52:1557) found strength training reduced sports injuries at a rate ratio of 0.338 (95% CI 0.238–0.480, I²=0%, high certainty) with a ~75% reduction in anterior knee pain — the highest-certainty finding in this area, and notably one about strength training generally rather than about screening.

**Dose is where this most often fails in practice.** The evidenced dose is roughly 20–30 minutes, 1–2×/week, sustained beyond 6 months (2025 NMT meta-analysis, 24 RCTs). A few minutes of band walks tacked onto the front of a squat session will not reach it — this needs its own standing weekly time allocation, budgeted like any other program component, which is a scheduling decision as much as an exercise-selection one. Adherence is the dominant moderator and is worth tracking as deliberately as load progression: high adherence (≥76% of prescribed sessions completed) was associated with a 64% ACL risk reduction in a dose-responsive relationship (*HSS Journal* 2023;19:154–162), and FIFA 11+ reviews report ~30–46% injury reduction explicitly conditional on adherence. A short circuit a client completes every week beats an elaborate one she skips when time is short. Worth adding if absent: a Nordic/eccentric hamstring component — a meta-analysis of 8,459 athletes (*BJSM* 2019;53:1362) found Nordic hamstring programs roughly halve hamstring-injury rates, among the largest single-exercise effects in this literature, and a distinct muscle group and mechanism from the Copenhagen plank's adductor focus rather than a substitute for it.

**Copenhagen adduction exercise.** A partner/bench eccentric adductor exercise producing ~108% MVIC EMG in the adductor longus. The Adductor Strengthening Programme (Harøy et al. 2018, BJSM, cluster-RCT, 35 teams/652 players) reduced groin problems by 41% over a season, done 3×/week preseason (6–8 wk) then 1×/week in-season, with 3 progression levels. Systematic reviews confirm it reliably improves eccentric hip-adduction strength (a key groin-injury risk factor) in a dose-dependent way; low-dose (2×/week for 6 weeks) improves squeeze strength but may not raise eccentric strength significantly (Dawkins 2021). Most data are in men; applicable to women but under-studied.

**Adductor:abductor ratio.** Balanced hip strength is protective; weakness in either — abductors for knee valgus/ACL, adductors for groin — flags risk. Screen with handheld dynamometry (squeeze test, side-lying abduction).

**Pelvic floor in loaded exercise.** Heavy lifting raises intra-abdominal pressure (IAP), amplified by the Valsalva maneuver; if pelvic floor muscles can't counter it, risk of stress urinary incontinence and prolapse rises. A crossover study in strength-trained nulliparous women (able to squat 1.2× and deadlift 1.5× bodyweight; 4×4 at 75–85% 1RM) examined acute PFM effects. A menstrual-cycle study found greater pelvic-floor displacement during menstruation than ovulation on ballistic lifting (6.0 vs 5.1 mm, p=0.03) — but pelvic-floor pre-contraction eliminated the difference. Practice: teach breath/bracing and pelvic-floor pre-contraction, progress load gradually, screen for symptoms (leaking, heaviness), and refer to pelvic-floor PT when symptomatic. Symptoms are common even in nulliparous athletes and are under-discussed.

**Diastasis recti (DRA).** Diagnosed by inter-recti distance (IRD); normal ~17–28 mm at 6 months postpartum; DRA often defined as >2.2–2.5 cm. Conservative exercise (deep core/transversus abdominis + pelvic floor, progressing to whole-abdominal work, plus breathing) produces statistically significant but often modest IRD reductions (meta-analyses: ~−6.8 to −8.1 mm; one review noted only ~0.4 cm vs usual care — of uncertain clinical significance). Comprehensive programs engaging both superficial and deep muscles, sometimes with biofeedback/breathing, outperform isolated approaches. The old blanket ban on rectus-abdominis work is not well supported; individualized loading with monitoring is appropriate.

**DRA prevalence and persistence — it is not a 6-week-resolving condition.** Sperstad, Tennfjord, Hilde & Ellström-Engh (2016, *British Journal of Sports Medicine* 50(17):1092–1096; n=300, Norwegian School of Sport Sciences/Akershus University Hospital) tracked DRA prevalence at 60% at 6 weeks postpartum, falling to 45.4% at 6 months and 32.6% at 12 months — meaning roughly a third of women still have DRA a full year out, not a population where the condition has quietly resolved by the standard "cleared at 6 weeks" milestone. Longer-term data go further: a 2024 cross-sectional study (*Scientific Reports*; n=1,000 women at five time points) found DRA (IRD >2 cm) still present in 36%, 31%, 22%, 26%, and 30% of women at 3, 5, 10, 20, and 30 years postpartum respectively (using the stricter IRD >3 cm criterion: 13%, 8%, 6%, 8%, 10%). Practical read: a client's "postpartum" status as a clinical consideration should not be assumed stale just because she is years past delivery — ask rather than assume it resolved.

**Postpartum return to training — the field has moved away from a single blanket clearance date.** The 2025 Canadian Guideline for Physical Activity, Sedentary Behaviour and Sleep Throughout the First Year Postpartum (a pan-Canadian Consensus Panel process, ~19,000 articles screened supporting 7 systematic reviews, published *British Journal of Sports Medicine* 2025, endorsed by ACSM, the Canadian Physiotherapy Association, and several other bodies — the first Canadian update on postpartum movement since 2003) recommends beginning or returning to moderate-to-vigorous physical activity within the first 12 weeks postpartum for mental-health benefit, starting with light-intensity movement (gentle walking, pelvic-floor muscle training) once bleeding/incisions allow, and progressing toward 120 min/week of MVPA spread across ≥4 days — explicitly including muscle-strengthening exercise alongside aerobic movement, not cardio with resistance training as an afterthought. This is a genuinely individualized, symptom-based progression model, not a fixed date — a physician's standard 6-week "cleared for exercise" note is a floor, not a ceiling, and does not by itself establish readiness for heavy compound loading. Practical markers worth checking before progressing intensity: leaking, pelvic pressure/heaviness, and visible midline doming or coning of the abdomen under load — the last of these is a coachable, observable signal a trainer can act on without attempting a diagnostic IRD measurement they aren't qualified to perform.

**Sequencing, not exclusion, is the evidence-based response to DRA — corroborating the point already made above.** Gluppe, Ellström Engh & Bø (2021, *Brazilian Journal of Physical Therapy* 25:664–675, systematic review with meta-analysis) found low-quality but positive evidence that BOTH transversus-abdominis (TrA) training AND curl-up training outperformed minimal intervention for reducing DRA — i.e., curl-up-pattern training is not disqualified by the evidence itself, it simply hasn't been shown to be uniquely necessary either (pelvic-floor-muscle training alone, by contrast, showed low-to-very-low-quality evidence of no benefit over minimal intervention in the same review). This maps directly onto this file's own Three-Zone-adjacent logic already used elsewhere for injury-adjacent populations: foundational deep-core/pelvic-floor control before reintroducing flexion-pattern loading, not a permanent ban on flexion work. The trials reviewed were mostly in women with mild DRA and the programs studied were heterogeneous in exercise type/dose, so no single protocol is established as superior — only that structured exercise beats no intervention.

**Genuine gap.** No RCT or systematic review found in this pass gives a specific %1RM, load, or fixed week-count threshold for when a postpartum client can safely progress to heavy compound loading (e.g., LIFTMOR-style >85% 1RM work) — the 2025 CSEP guideline's own framing is deliberately individualized and symptom-based rather than threshold-based, so stating a specific number here would misrepresent the evidence. Progress load the same RIR-governed way as any other client, gated by the symptom checks above rather than a fixed timeline.

**Osteoarthritis (knee & hip) and resistance training — a real gap this file did not previously cover.** Knee/hip OA is extremely common in this client population, and the default coaching instinct — lighten load or avoid the joint pattern around a painful/diagnosed joint — runs directly counter to current evidence, which treats resistance training as core first-line OA management, not a risk to train defensively around.

*Prevalence and the estrogen link.* OA disproportionately affects women, and the gap widens sharply after menopause. A 2025 narrative synthesis in *npj Women's Health* ("The intersection of aging and estrogen in osteoarthritis," Nature Portfolio, 2025;3:15) reports radiographic knee OA roughly three-fold more common in women aged 45–64 than in age-matched men — a notably larger ratio than the "~2×" figure sometimes quoted for OA generally, which appears to reflect symptomatic-OA definitions rather than radiographic ones; the two definitions produce genuinely different ratios in this literature and it's worth being precise about which one a given figure refers to rather than treating "2×" and "3×" as interchangeable. A companion 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis (*Journal of Menopausal Medicine*, 6 human + 5 ovariectomized-animal-model studies, literature search through March 2025) found postmenopausal women show higher OA prevalence and severity than premenopausal women, and that estrogen's mechanistic role — anti-inflammatory effects, chondrocyte-metabolism regulation, protection against oxidative stress — is well supported at the cellular/animal-model level; one hormone-therapy-association data point in the surrounding literature puts OA prevalence roughly 30% lower in women on menopausal hormone therapy, though that is an associational finding, not a controlled causal trial. Be precise about the evidentiary hierarchy: "menopause raises OA risk, with a biologically plausible estrogen-driven mechanism" is well-supported; "here is exactly how much of that rise is estrogen loss versus general aging" is not yet settled at the human level, even though the mechanism is compelling at the molecular/animal level. This directly reinforces — without newly inventing — this file's existing "joint lubrication" language elsewhere in Brace Life's philosophy framing around declining estrogen; it did not previously have a citation behind it, and now does.

*Standing exercise guideline.* The 2019 ACR/Arthritis Foundation Guideline for the Management of Osteoarthritis of the Hand, Hip, and Knee (Kolasinski et al., *Arthritis & Rheumatology* 2020;72(2):220–233 — still the most current version located in this search; no 2025/2026 update found) strongly recommends exercise as first-line management for knee and hip OA, naming land-based strengthening, neuromuscular training, walking, and aquatic exercise with no established hierarchy among them, and notes supervised exercise produces better outcomes than unsupervised. This is a broad, modality-agnostic "exercise works" recommendation rather than a specific loading prescription — the next two findings supply the loading specifics the ACR guideline itself doesn't.

*Dosing specifics — symptom-matched, not one-size-fits-all.* A 2025 Bayesian network meta-analysis (46 RCTs, 3,463 participants, *BMC Musculoskeletal Disorders*, PROSPERO CRD42024535806) found high-speed resistance training the single most effective modality across pain (SMD −1.35), stiffness (SMD −1.26), and function (SMD 1.70) combined. Its dose-response analysis is the more actionable finding for programming: moderate-intensity RT (43–47% 1RM) sustained over 35–37 weeks with a higher weekly rep volume (610–640 reps/week) was optimal for pain (SMD −0.76) and function (SMD 1.29) specifically; for stiffness specifically, a shorter, higher-rep protocol (12 weeks, ~1,200 reps/week; SMD −1.11) performed better. In other words, the right OA prescription depends on which symptom is actually limiting the client — a sustained moderate-intensity block for a pain/function complaint, a shorter higher-rep block for a stiffness-predominant complaint — not one default OA protocol.

*Heavier loading is not disqualified by OA or joint laxity — directly relevant to this file's own postmenopausal-ligament-laxity framing (section 5, ACL discussion above).* The PROHIP trial (Denmark; hip OA patients with an existing surgical indication; secondary analysis on training load and pain response published 2025, open-access via PMC) found patients tolerated progressively higher resistance-training loads across a 12-week program while maintaining low pre-/post-session pain intensity — the opposite direction of "this joint is too far gone to load heavily." A separate case series (Henriksen, Junge, Bojsen-Møller & Juul-Kristensen, *Translational Sports Medicine* 2022 — note: 2022, not more recent, the actual publication date for this specific cohort study, confirmed via direct search) supervised 16 young women with generalized joint hypermobility and knee pain through 12 weeks of heavy resistance training (leg press, calf raise, leg extension, leg curl, forward lunge; 2×/week) and found improved lower-extremity strength and — notable for a population whose joint laxity is often treated as a default reason to avoid heavy loading — no major adverse events, alongside gains reported in proprioception and patellar tendon stiffness. Flag the population honestly: mean age 24.2, hypermobility-specific, not this file's postmenopausal-women bracket — cited here as counter-evidence to the "ligament laxity makes heavy loading unsafe" assumption in principle, not as a bracket-matched replication for a 55-year-old client.

*The boundary this file should be honest about: training is not a substitute for an already-indicated surgery.* Frydendal T. et al., "Total Hip Replacement or Resistance Training for Severe Hip Osteoarthritis," *New England Journal of Medicine* 2024;391(17):1610–1620 — a multicenter RCT, 109 patients aged 50+ with severe hip OA and an existing surgical indication, randomized to total hip replacement (n=53) or supervised resistance training (n=56) — found hip replacement produced a significantly greater improvement in the primary outcome (Oxford Hip Score, pain/function) at 6 months than resistance training alone, even though physical activity levels improved comparably in both arms. Practical implication: resistance training's strength as OA management does not extend to matching a surgical outcome once a surgeon has already indicated replacement — that specific conversation belongs with her orthopedist, not a training program positioned as an alternative to it. A related, more recent systematic review/meta-analysis (knee-arthroplasty-specific; ahead-of-print/2026, covering both preoperative high-intensity strength training and resistance training relative to subsequent knee replacement) found resistance training has a genuine role on both sides of a replacement — greater strength gains with higher-intensity preoperative work, better postoperative pain/function/mobility outcomes with programs of adequate duration — meaning training and surgery are complementary across a replacement timeline, not competing options once surgery is actually indicated.

*Practical read for ICONS programming.* Don't default to reducing load or avoiding a joint pattern just because a client reports an OA diagnosis or joint pain — that instinct runs counter to the evidence above. Screen at intake for which joint, diagnosed vs. undiagnosed pain, and whether a surgical-indication conversation is already underway; a client already in that conversation should have expectations calibrated to the NEJM finding above, not led to expect training alone will match what a surgeon has already flagged as replacement-indicated. Program to the client's limiting symptom (sustained moderate-intensity work for a pain/function complaint, shorter higher-rep blocks for a stiffness-predominant complaint) rather than one blanket OA prescription. Pain occurring during or persisting meaningfully after a session — as distinct from a client's pre-existing baseline pain — is the practical, coachable regression signal, the same "does the pattern hold up under load" judgment already used elsewhere in this section for postpartum midline doming and squat-depth breakdown. Diagnosis, imaging interpretation, and surgical timing stay an external referral.

**Genuine gap.** No RCT or systematic review located in this pass specifically replicates LIFTMOR's exact bone-loading protocol (>85% 1RM, 5×5, 2×/week, supervised) in a confirmed-OA, postmenopausal population — the dosing evidence above (the 46-RCT network meta-analysis, PROHIP) is drawn from mixed-age, mixed-sex OA RCT pools, not from this file's specific 40–65 bracket population. Treat the guidance above as directly applicable, well-evidenced OA-specific dosing — not as a bracket-matched replication of LIFTMOR the way the core Bone Loading discussion in section 2 is.

### 6. Nutrition Science for Women in Strength Training

**Protein synthesis, leucine, and sex.** Basal plasma leucine is ~15% lower in women than men, but MPS responses to feeding are similar in young men and women. Older women show anabolic resistance (blunted MPS response to feeding). Leucine-enriched essential amino acids (as little as 3 g EAA at 40% leucine) can stimulate MPS equivalently to 20 g whey in older women at rest and after exercise (Bukhari/Phillips work), useful for lower-appetite older clients.

**Daily and per-meal protein targets.** Morton et al. (2018, BJSM) meta-analysis (49 studies, 1,863 participants): "Protein supplementation beyond total protein intakes of 1.62 g/kg/day resulted in no further RET-induced gains in FFM" (breakpoint 1.62 g/kg/day, 95% CI 1.03–2.20); supplementation raised 1RM strength by 2.49 kg and fat-free mass by 0.30 kg, with effect "reduced with increasing age." Practically: 1.6 g/kg/day is the well-supported floor for training women, with up to ~2.2 g/kg justified by context rather than by age (see below), distributed as roughly 0.3 g/kg/meal across ~4 meals spaced 3–4 hours apart. A pre-sleep dose of 30–40 g of slow-digesting protein (e.g. casein) is a low-friction way to reach a daily total, and plant-based clients should add roughly 10% to whatever target otherwise applies. Sims recommends the higher end (~2.0–2.4 g/kg/day) for peri/postmenopausal women plus a substantial post-workout dose (40–60 g within ~45 min), though at least one RCT (Nabuco 2017) did not find added lean-mass benefit from higher protein in postmenopausal women.

**What actually triggers the higher end — corrected 8/18/2026, and this supersedes rather than refines what this file said before.** Until this pass, both this document and ICONS's compact science layer treated the top of the protein range as *age*-triggered — an escalation at ~50, or on an ALST flag. The most current female-specific synthesis does not support the age half of that. D'Souza AC, Greyvenstein D, Newbold JP & Phillips SM, "Muscle Protein Metabolism and Protein Requirements for Female Athletes: Aligning Science With Sex-Specific Needs," *Gatorade Sports Science Institute Sports Science Exchange* 2025;38(270), targets 1.4–1.6 g/kg/day for female athletes, emphasises even distribution every 3–4 hours "regardless of menstrual cycle phase," and reserves higher intakes for **energy restriction or heavy training load** specifically. On menopause it is candid in a way that matters here: preliminary data indicate a pronounced decrease in skeletal muscle protein balance across the pre- to peri-menopausal transition, plausibly reflecting anabolic resistance — *and* there has been **no research in postmenopausal master female athletes on which to base age- and sex-specific protein recommendations**. That is an evidence *absence*, not a positive finding of equivalence, and it should be represented as such rather than rounded in either direction: there is a mechanistically plausible case for higher intake with age, and no trial base to set a number from. Direct RCT evidence for pushing intake up by age alone is thin — 1.2 vs. 0.8 g/kg/day in resistance-training postmenopausal women produced only a small functional-capacity difference with no difference in strength or lean-mass quality (*Nutrients* 2019;11:1323) — and geriatric consensus bodies (PROT-AGE, ESPEN) recommend ≥1.0–1.2 g/kg/day for healthy older adults generally, well below what an age-banded escalation implies. Practical read: **hold 1.6 g/kg/day as the baseline for a training woman at any age, and move up toward 2.2 g/kg for a genuine energy deficit, a heavy training load, or a low-lean-mass flag — not for a birthday.** Morton et al.'s "reduced with increasing age" effect describes a *diminishing return* on supplementation with age, which is not the same claim as a higher requirement, and is worth not over-reading in either direction.

The per-meal figure also came down in this pass, from ~0.4 to ~0.3 g/kg. The 0.4 figure sat above both the ISSN's ~0.25 g/kg serving guidance and the ~0.31 g/kg/meal MPS-maximising dose identified in the GSSI review — it was a defensible upper-bound reading of Schoenfeld & Aragon (2018) but not the central estimate. Related: the widely repeated ~2.5–3 g leucine-per-meal threshold could not be traced to a primary source in this pass and should be treated as an approximate heuristic, not a protocol-grade number.

**Carbohydrate timing across the cycle.** Weak evidence. Sims suggests slightly higher carbohydrate availability/pre-loading before hard luteal-phase sessions (progesterone shifts metabolism toward fat oxidation and impairs glycogen access), but this is a small, practical tweak, not a strong effect.

**Micronutrients for active women.** Iron deficiency affects roughly 3–11% of male athletes vs 15–35% of female athletes; some elite cohorts report suboptimal iron status in ~52% of women using a 35 ng/mL cutoff. The RDI for iron is ~2.5× higher in women. Monitor ferritin (deficiency commonly flagged <30 µg/L per Swiss Society of Sports Medicine consensus) rather than serum iron; pair iron with vitamin C, separate from calcium/coffee/tea. Vitamin D insufficiency affects ~33–42% of female athletes, and vitamin D and iron status are interrelated (VDD roughly triples odds of iron deficiency in female athletes). Calcium and vitamin D are critical for bone, especially with low estrogen/amenorrhea. Magnesium supports many active women who run low.

**Female athlete triad / RED-S.** Low energy availability (LEA) is the root cause of both the Triad (disordered eating/low EA, menstrual dysfunction, low BMD — ACSM 1992/2007) and the broader RED-S (IOC 2014, updated 2018; multi-system: metabolic, hormonal, bone, immune, cardiovascular, psychological, protein synthesis). Screen with the LEAF-Q (Br J Sports Med 2014) and the IOC RED-S CAT2 (2023), ideally at annual physicals. Amenorrhea, stress fractures, and underperformance are red flags. This is a major, under-recognized risk in lean-focused and high-volume female training.

**Creatine.** 3–5 g/day monohydrate; no loading needed. Women have lower baseline creatine stores, potentially making them more responsive. Insist on monohydrate specifically — the ISSN states other forms (HCl, buffered, etc.) are not superior. Safety is unusually well established: no compelling evidence of harm at doses up to 30 g/day for five years in healthy or clinical populations, with weight gain (~1 lb transient water) the only consistently reported effect, plus FDA GRAS status (2020). Benefits on strength and power are the best-supported, especially combined with resistance training in older women. Hall et al. (2025, *J Int Soc Sports Nutr*, St. Olaf College) — 15 women (mean age 54; 5 perimenopausal, 10 postmenopausal) taking 5 g creatine daily + twice-weekly strength training for 14 weeks — found significant increases in lower-body strength (isometric concentric extensor peak torque at 60°, p<0.05) and improved sleep quality in perimenopausal women (p=0.0181).

Three refinements added 8/18/2026, none of which change the headline verdict — creatine remains the best-evidenced supplement in this file:

*Saturation timing, said plainly to the client.* At 3–5 g/day with no loading phase, muscle saturation takes roughly 4 weeks (ISSN 2021: a loading phase "is not required" at 3–5 g/day, or 0.1 g/kg, for a minimum of 4 weeks to reach comparable saturation). Frame the slower onset as a deliberate adherence trade-off rather than an oversight — GI distress during loading measurably reduces adherence. Taking it with food, ideally carbohydrate or carbohydrate plus protein, appears to increase muscular uptake, though performance outcomes may not differ.

*The training pairing is not optional messaging.* Creatine **without** resistance training did not increase lean mass in postmenopausal women over two years. It should always be presented as an adjunct to training, never as a standalone intervention — which is also the honest answer to a client hoping the supplement does the work.

*The cognition evidence is real but not at this dose.* Memory, attention, and processing speed improved in 5 of 6 reviewed older-adult trials (*Nutrition Reviews* systematic review, 2025) — but brain creatine uptake is slow and limited (only a ~5–15% rise with supplementation), and the protocols producing those effects used substantially higher doses than 3–5 g/day: typically 15–20 g/day loading for 3–7 days then 5–10 g/day maintenance, or single studies at 5 g/day for 6 weeks, 8 g/day for 5 days, or 20 g/day for 7 days. Marketing ICONS's standard 3–5 g/day dose on cognitive grounds without that caveat overstates what the dose has been shown to do.

**Creatine and bone — mixed, and the resolution is a dose question (resolves the 8/12/2026 cross-reference note formerly held here).** This file previously listed "possible bone benefits" as a flat claim; that was the pre-correction framing and is retired. The evidence genuinely conflicts. Postmenopausal women taking 0.1 g/kg/day alongside heavy resistance training for 52 weeks attenuated femoral-neck and hip bone-mineral loss; a well-designed 2-year RCT in 237 postmenopausal women at 0.14 g/kg found **no** BMD effect, though proximal femur geometry improved; a 24-week trial found appendicular lean-mass gains with no bone effect. The dose detail is the interesting part and is easy to miss: 0.1 g/kg/day is roughly 6–7 g/day for a 65 kg woman — *above* the standard 3–5 g band — so the trials showing a bone signal were not testing the dose most clients take. A defensible position is to keep 3–5 g/day as the general default, offer an optional body-mass-scaled tier (0.10–0.14 g/kg/day) to a client whose primary goal is bone, and coach creatine as a strength-and-cognition supplement with a possible, dose-dependent bone upside rather than as a bone intervention. Heavy loading (section 2) is the settled bone protocol; creatine is not a substitute for it.

**Collagen for connective tissue — timing narrowed and claims downgraded 8/18/2026.** Keith Baar's lab (Shaw et al., *AJCN* 2017;105(1):136–143) gave 15 g collagen/gelatin + ~50 mg vitamin C (48 mg in the trial) and roughly doubled blood markers of collagen synthesis (PINP), with serum drawn an hour after the 15 g dose increasing collagen content and mechanical strength in an engineered-ligament model. Both the nutrient and the mechanical load are required.

Two corrections to how this file previously presented it. **First, timing: 45–60 minutes pre-load, not 30–60.** Shaw's actual protocol dosed **one hour** before exercise for the 15 g dose; the authors themselves noted a smaller 5 g dose would have been better timed at 30 minutes. The 30-minute end of the old window was never studied at the dose ICONS recommends, so quoting it as an equivalent option overstated the protocol's flexibility. **Second, the framing moves from acute-ergogenic to chronic-structural.** This is a 12+ week connective-tissue support protocol taken consistently on training days, not a pre-workout performance aid. A January 2026 umbrella review (16 meta-analyses, 113 RCTs, 7,983 participants) describes collagen's effect as "chronic and structural rather than acutely ergogenic," finding a positive effect on tendon *morphology* (SMD 0.65) but at low certainty (only 4 RCTs, 127 participants) and **no** effect on tendon mechanical properties; a separate meta-analysis of 13 RCTs (450 participants) found collagen peptides do not further improve musculoskeletal *performance* when added to exercise (Kirmse et al., *Dtsch Z Sportmed* 2024;75:179–188). The "doubles collagen synthesis markers" line is a real acute biomarker finding, but it should not be presented as a performance or outcome claim.

Dosing context: structural tendon benefits in trials generally used 15–30 g/day; a 2025 RCT (Miyamoto, *MSSE*) found 10 g/day over 16 weeks increased Achilles stiffness and explosive strength; higher doses (30 g) may further raise acute synthesis vs 15 g in trained young men.

**Rank collagen below creatine and protein in the supplement hierarchy, and note the sex-data problem.** Creatine rests on ISSN position stands and protein on multiple large meta-analyses; collagen rests on a small number of tendon-specific RCTs. The gap is sharper than usual for a document scoped to women: the foundational Shaw et al. protocol was run in **eight healthy male subjects**, and only 155 of 768 participants in the largest supporting review were women. This is one of the weakest sex-representation profiles of any recommendation in this file and should be stated when the protocol is offered, not buried. Collagen supports but does not replace loading and rehab.

### 7. Recovery & Sleep in Women

**Sleep across the cycle.** Subjective sleep quality reliably worsens in the late-luteal/premenstrual phase; objective (polysomnography) changes are smaller and less consistent, but the clearest findings are reduced REM and reduced slow-wave sleep in the luteal phase plus elevated core temperature (~0.3–0.7°C from progesterone) impairing sleep-onset heat dissipation. Sleep apnea may worsen in the luteal phase (one cohort: AHI 6.1/h follicular vs 14.3/h luteal, p=0.033). Postmenopausal women have lower HRV and more sleep disruption.

**HRV monitoring — observational, not a decision input (downgraded 8/18/2026).** The descriptive physiology is solid: in naturally cycling women, HRV (RMSSD/SDNN) is generally higher early in the cycle and declines in the luteal phase; Schmalenberger et al. showed higher-than-usual progesterone predicts lower-than-usual HRV, with the lowest values mid-luteal (no significant estradiol effect). The interpretive point still stands and matters — a woman's HRV baseline naturally oscillates across the cycle, so reading it against a flat baseline invites mistaking a normal luteal dip for overreaching.

What does *not* hold up is using HRV to drive training decisions. A 2024 RCT (n=21 older women, 7 weeks) comparing HRV-individualized recovery scheduling against fixed scheduling found no significant between-group advantage on **any** strength, hypertrophy, or function outcome measured. That is a direct test of the thing coaches actually want HRV for, and it came back null. Treat HRV as an optional observational metric a client may find interesting — worth explaining so she doesn't misread her own luteal dip — and keep symptom logs plus RIR/RPE as the operative autoregulation signals. This is consistent with, not a departure from, section 1's conclusion that individual symptom tracking beats any phase- or metric-driven scheme.

**Cortisol response.** Sex differences are stressor-dependent. Kirschbaum et al. (1992, Psychosomatic Medicine) found men's cortisol responses to psychological stress were 1.5–2× higher than women's, but responses to exercise-to-exhaustion and CRH challenge were similar between sexes. Roberts/Nuckols/Krieger (2020) noted males show reduced serum cortisol with chronic resistance training whereas females do not. Direction varies with acute vs chronic stress — report the specific context rather than over-interpreting single readings.

**Active recovery.** Strongest-evidence protocols: low-intensity movement (Zone 2 walking/cycling), adequate protein and total energy, and sleep hygiene (especially in the late-luteal phase). Zone 2 is a recovery adjunct, not a substitute for resistance training.

### 8. Psychological & Behavioral Factors

**Self-efficacy.** Holloway, Beuter & Duda (1988, J Applied Social Psychology): 12 weeks of strength training in untrained adolescent girls increased strength +40% and generalized to greater "confrontation efficacy" and life self-efficacy vs controls — early evidence that lifting builds transferable confidence in women. A 2024 Frontiers in Psychology barbell-training study found significant gains in mastery and self-efficacy when participants received rich, individualized progress feedback.

**Load-progression hesitancy.** Women (and novices generally) tend to self-select loads below what's optimal for adaptation. Ratamess et al. (2008) found women who worked with a trainer selected significantly higher loads than those training alone. State anxiety decreases at 40–60% 1RM but rises above 70% 1RM (Bartholomew; Focht), so coaching through the psychological barrier to heavy loads is key. Coaching levers: competent spotting, safety bars/racks, a culture of encouragement, and individualized progress tracking.

**Coaching language & body image.** Progress-based, mastery-oriented framing (tracking strength/reps/load PRs) builds self-efficacy better than appearance-based framing. Group/partner training increases adherence, enjoyment, and confidence (Spink & Carron 1994). Emphasize capability and function over aesthetics.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Foundation (weeks 0–8):**
- Train all major muscle groups 2–3×/week, compound movements, full range of motion.
- Teach bracing, breathing, and pelvic-floor coordination before loading heavy. Screen for incontinence/prolapse symptoms and diastasis (IRD). Use the single-leg squat and drop-landing as *technique* tools, not as injury-risk screens — and do not gate the corrective work on them (section 5).
- Establish protein at ≥1.6 g/kg/day, ~0.3 g/kg/meal across ~4 meals spaced 3–4 h. Check ferritin and vitamin D.
- Start creatine 3–5 g/day (no loading); set the expectation that saturation takes ~4 weeks.
- Establish baseline grip strength (dynamometer) and a five-times chair-stand alongside any body-composition scan — these sit above lean mass in the consensus sarcopenia hierarchy and cost far less (section 2).
- Coach load selection actively — women systematically under-load themselves; use RIR/RPE and progress deliberately. Calibrate her RIR reporting before trusting it for load decisions: have her call RIR on a submaximal set, then take that set to true failure and record the discrepancy, repeating until error is ≤1 rep on two consecutive sessions.

**Stage 2 — Build (weeks 8–24):**
- Progress toward heavy loading (>85% 1RM, 5×5, **supervised**) for strength/bone on key lifts, after a technique-first ramp-in and a risk-stratification check (prior vertebral/low-trauma fracture or very low BMD → physiotherapist input first). Add impact/jump work 2×/week — it is part of the bone stimulus, not an optional extra. Avoid loaded spinal flexion.
- Exploit women's fatigue resistance: use higher weekly set volumes (hypertrophy ≥10 sets/muscle/week) and somewhat shorter rest where appropriate.
- Run the neuromuscular/corrective circuit as a standing, budgeted component — 20–30 min, 1–2×/week, sustained beyond 6 months, for every client rather than only screen-positive ones. Track session completion against a ≥76% adherence target. Include hip-abductor/glute-med work, Copenhagen adductor work, and a Nordic/eccentric hamstring component.
- Add collagen 15 g + ~50 mg vitamin C, 45–60 min pre-loading if managing tendon/joint issues — as a 12+ week structural support protocol, not a performance aid.

**Stage 3 — Individualize & sustain:**
- Do not build the program around menstrual phases. Train hard year-round; autoregulate around individual symptoms tracked over ≥3 consecutive cycles. Calendar/app-predicted phase is not an acceptable input — phase calculated from dates alone is inaccurate.
- Peri/postmenopause: heavy resistance ≥3×/week, impact/sprint work, and a discussion of HRT with her physician (it may enhance the training response). This is the highest-yield window for preventing bone/muscle loss. **Do not escalate protein on age alone** — hold 1.6 g/kg/day as the baseline and move toward 2.2 g/kg for a genuine energy deficit, a heavy training load, or a low-lean-mass flag (see section 6; this reverses the age-tier guidance this file previously carried).
- Treat HRV as optional and observational — explain the normal luteal dip so she doesn't misread it, but don't schedule off it. Prioritize sleep hygiene in the late-luteal week.
- Screen for RED-S/low energy availability (LEAF-Q / IOC RED-S CAT2) in lean-focused or high-volume trainees. Absent, irregular, or newly-lost periods warrant medical referral rather than a programming adjustment — and note that resumption of menses alone does not confirm ovulatory recovery.

**Benchmarks that change the plan:**
- Ferritin <30 µg/L → investigate/treat iron deficiency before pushing volume.
- Grip strength <16 kg → the *primary* EWGSOP2 flag, ahead of any scan number; confirm with ALM index <5.5 kg/m², and grade severity with gait speed ≤0.8 m/s or five-times chair-stand >15 s. Prioritize hypertrophy/strength, protein, and refer. (Quote these EWGSOP2 values as such — grip <20 kg and gait <1.0 m/s belong to EWGSOP1 and AWGS respectively, and mixing working groups produces a threshold no consensus body actually publishes.) Never apply 7.0 kg/m² to a woman — that is the male cutoff.
- VAT → read as a personal trend, not a threshold verdict. If a number must be interpreted, elevated-risk values in women run nearer ≥106 cm² than the commonly quoted 100 (Kelley et al. 2003), and a midlife-women-specific analysis puts the optimal *range* at 46.5–116.6 cm² once bone risk at the low end is accounted for (Tan et al. 2026) — lower is not uniformly better. Waist circumference measured to protocol, against the IAS/ICCR female thresholds (≥80/≥90/≥105/≥115 cm, BMI-stratified), is the better clinical-facing metric. Either way: emphasize resistance training + nutrition.
- Left/right segmental difference → act on a **relative** gap ≥10%, confirmed against a functional single-leg test where possible; a sub-pound absolute difference is within the scanner's own measurement error, particularly in the legs.
- Incontinence/prolapse/DRA symptoms under load → reduce IAP demand, refer to pelvic-floor PT.
- Amenorrhea/stress fracture → screen RED-S immediately; address energy availability first.
- Postpartum client, any time frame → do not treat a physician's 6-week clearance as sufficient for heavy compound loading on its own; confirm current symptom status (leaking, pelvic pressure/heaviness, visible midline doming/coning under load) before progressing, and sequence deep-core/pelvic-floor foundational work ahead of flexion-pattern loading rather than banning it outright. A "postpartum" flag does not expire on a fixed timeline — DRA can persist for years; ask rather than assume it resolved.
- Healthy, non-deficient, non-osteoporotic client asks about calcium/vitamin D supplementation for bone → don't default to recommending it; USPSTF 2024 draft found no net fracture-prevention benefit at standard doses, and calcium carries an unresolved CVD-risk signal in postmenopausal women. Confirmed osteoporosis, prior fracture, malabsorption, or confirmed deficiency → follow her prescriber's guidance, not this general framing. Cardiac-flagged client → extra caution on calcium specifically; route to her physician rather than encourage it.
- Client reports a knee/hip OA diagnosis or joint pain → do not default to reducing load or avoiding the pattern; screen which joint, diagnosed vs. undiagnosed, and whether a surgical-indication conversation is already underway. Program to her limiting symptom (sustained moderate-intensity for pain/function; shorter, higher-rep blocks for stiffness). Pain during/persisting after a session (not her pre-existing baseline pain) is the regression signal. A surgeon-indicated replacement already on the table → calibrate expectations to the 2024 NEJM finding (surgery outperformed training alone at 6 months) rather than implying training will match a surgical outcome; refer to her orthopedist for that specific conversation.

## Caveats

- Menstrual-cycle science is genuinely contested. Popular practitioners (Sims) and some meta-analyses claim phase effects; the highest-quality umbrella review (Phillips lab 2023) finds the evidence too weak to program around. Weight the umbrella review more heavily on methodology, but reasonable experts disagree, and individual symptom-based adjustment remains sensible.
- Much foundational research is on men or young women; older-women and perimenopausal RCTs are fewer, and some findings (e.g., higher-protein benefit in postmenopausal women, cycle-based nutrition) are extrapolated or conflicting.
- Cortisol/HRV sex differences are context-dependent (acute vs chronic stress); avoid over-interpreting single readings.
- Body-composition tools differ in accuracy: DXA is the practical reference for fat/lean/VAT/ALM; 3D scanners (Styku) are best for tracking shape/girth trends, not absolute body-fat criterion measures; BIA cutoffs differ from DXA. Two specifics worth carrying separately (section 4): the scanner's *segmental* output is its least reliable, so left/right comparisons deserve real skepticism; and DXA is itself only a practical reference, not a criterion — against a true four-compartment model, optical scanning overestimates %BF by ~5 points, and part of its apparent agreement with DXA is built into the fact that it is calibrated to DXA in the first place.
- **Male reference values are applied to women more often than anyone intends, including in this document's own history.** The ALM index case (section 2) is the clearest: 7.0 kg/m² is the male sarcopenia cutoff, and it circulated inside this system as a female "optimal" target until 8/17–8/18/2026. The same trap exists for waist circumference (≥88 cm female vs. ≥102 cm male, plus a separate BMI-stratified IAS/ICCR set), for grip strength (<16 kg female vs. <27 kg male), and for VAT, where sex-neutral thresholds understate the female-specific values. When a threshold in this file is quoted anywhere downstream, the sex it was derived in should travel with it.
- Several thresholds in wide circulation are **weaker than their confident presentation suggests**, and this file should keep saying so: EWGSOP2 states outright that some of its own cut-offs "are arbitrary at this time"; SDOC dropped DXA lean mass from the sarcopenia definition entirely; no consensus body publishes a single VAT cutoff; and no source located anywhere gives a calibration or rescan interval for optical scanning, meaning any studio cadence is an internal policy rather than a standard.
- Sex representation in the underlying trials is uneven even where the recommendation is aimed squarely at women. The collagen protocol is the sharpest example — the foundational Shaw et al. dosing study was run in eight men, and women were 155 of 768 participants in the largest supporting review (section 6). Creatine's bone data, by contrast, is postmenopausal-women-specific but internally conflicting. Neither is a reason to drop the recommendation; both are reasons not to present it as settled.
- Supplement effects are modest: creatine and collagen have real but bounded benefits and do not substitute for training, protein sufficiency, and sleep.
- Injury-prevention percentages (ACL, groin) come largely from youth/athletic cohorts and may not transfer identically to older recreational women; the ~2.8× ACL incidence ratio (team ball sports) is lower than the often-quoted "4–6×," which reflected specific high-risk sport subsets.
- Movement *screening* and movement *training* have very different evidentiary standing, and conflating them is easy. The neuromuscular training itself has high-certainty support; the visual screens used to decide who receives it do not predict injury at all in the largest prospective tests (section 5). A finding that a screen fails is not a finding that the underlying mechanism is fictional — dynamic valgus is real and coachable — only that a trainer cannot forecast from it.
